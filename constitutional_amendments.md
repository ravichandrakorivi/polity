# Important Constitutional Amendments of India

## 1. Constitutional amendment: Basic understanding

A constitutional amendment is a formal change to the Constitution of India. It may add, modify or repeal constitutional provisions.

The principal procedure for amending the Constitution is laid down in Article 368, contained in Part XX.

### 1.1 Three methods of changing constitutional provisions

| Method                                | Majority required                                                                                | Examples                                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Simple majority                       | Majority of members present and voting in Parliament                                             | Creation or abolition of State Legislative Councils under Article 169; formation of new States under Articles 3–4 |
| Special majority under Article 368    | Majority of total membership of each House and at least two-thirds of members present and voting | Most constitutional amendments, including Fundamental Rights                                                      |
| Special majority + State ratification | Article 368 special majority in each House + ratification by at least half of State Legislatures | Amendments affecting specified federal provisions                                                                 |

Important: Changes made through the simple-majority route are not treated as constitutional amendments under Article 368.

### 1.2 Important procedural points

- A Constitutional Amendment Bill may be introduced in either House of Parliament.
- It may be introduced by a minister or a private member.
- Prior permission of the President is not required for introducing such a Bill.
- Both Houses must pass it separately by the required special majority.
- There is no provision for a joint sitting to resolve disagreement between the Houses.
- State ratification is required only for amendments involving the specified federal provisions.
- Where ratification is required, at least half of the States must ratify the amendment.
- The President must give assent to a Constitutional Amendment Bill duly passed in accordance with Article 368.

### 1.3 Basic Structure Doctrine

In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), the Supreme Court held that Parliament may amend any part of the Constitution but cannot destroy or alter its basic structure.

Examples of basic-structure features recognised in judicial decisions include:

- Supremacy of the Constitution
- Rule of law
- Judicial review
- Separation of powers
- Federalism
- Secularism
- Democracy and republican government
- Independence of the judiciary
- Free and fair elections

The basic structure is not contained in a single exhaustive constitutional list.

## 2. Important amendments: 1951–1978

### 2.1 First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951

Major provisions

- Added Article 15(4), enabling special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes and SCs/STs.
- Expanded the grounds for reasonable restrictions on freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(2).
- Added Articles 31A and 31B to protect certain laws, particularly land-reform measures.
- Introduced the Ninth Schedule.

Prelims points

- The Ninth Schedule was introduced by the 1st Amendment, not the 42nd Amendment.
- Laws placed in the Ninth Schedule are not automatically immune from all judicial review.
- In I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), the Supreme Court held that laws added to the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are subject to basic-structure review.

### 2.2 Seventh Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956

Major provisions

- Facilitated reorganisation of States following the States Reorganisation Act, 1956.
- Abolished the earlier classification of States into Part A, Part B and Part C States.
- Established the present broad classification of States and Union Territories.
- Made important changes concerning High Courts and State administration.

Prelims point: The 7th Amendment is closely associated with the linguistic reorganisation of States in 1956.

### 2.3 Twenty-Fourth Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971

Major provisions

- Expressly affirmed Parliament's power to amend any provision of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights.
- Amended Articles 13 and 368.
- Made presidential assent to a duly passed Constitutional Amendment Bill mandatory.

Prelims point: It was enacted following Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967), which had restricted Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights.

### 2.4 Twenty-Fifth Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971

Major provisions

- Introduced Article 31C.
- Originally protected laws giving effect to the Directive Principles in Article 39(b) and Article 39(c) against challenges under Articles 14 and 19, subject to judicial interpretation.
- Replaced the expression compensation with amount in the then Article 31(2).

Important: The 42nd Amendment later attempted to extend Article 31C protection to all Directive Principles. That extension was invalidated in Minerva Mills (1980).

### 2.5 Twenty-Sixth Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971

Major provisions

- Abolished the privy purses and privileges of former rulers of princely States.
- Removed constitutional recognition of former rulers.
- Inserted Article 363A and omitted Articles 291 and 362.

### 2.6 Thirty-Sixth Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975

Major provision

- Made Sikkim a full-fledged State of India.
- Inserted Article 371F, providing special constitutional arrangements for Sikkim.

Prelims distinction: The 35th Amendment (1974) had introduced Sikkim's associate-State arrangement; the 36th Amendment made it a full State.

### 2.7 Forty-Second Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976

Frequently called the Mini-Constitution

One of the most extensive amendments to the Indian Constitution.

Major provisions

1. Added Socialist, Secular and Integrity to the Preamble.
2. Introduced Fundamental Duties through Article 51A and Part IVA.
3. Added Directive Principles:
   - Article 39A — Equal justice and free legal aid.
   - Article 43A — Participation of workers in management of industries.
   - Article 48A — Protection and improvement of environment and safeguarding forests and wildlife.
4. Transferred five subjects from the State List to the Concurrent List:
   - Education
   - Forests
   - Weights and measures
   - Protection of wild animals and birds
   - Administration of justice; constitution and organisation of all courts except the Supreme Court and High Courts
5. Established administrative tribunals through Articles 323A and 323B.
6. Attempted to restrict judicial review and strengthen Parliament's amending powers.
7. Extended the normal term of the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies from five to six years (subsequently reversed).

Prelims points

- The original Constitution did not contain Fundamental Duties.
- The 42nd Amendment introduced 10 Fundamental Duties.
- The 11th Fundamental Duty was added by the 86th Amendment.
- Some provisions of the 42nd Amendment were subsequently reversed or invalidated.

### 2.8 Forty-Fourth Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978

Major provisions

- Restored the normal term of the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies to five years.
- Removed the Right to Property from the list of Fundamental Rights.
- Inserted Article 300A, protecting property as a constitutional right.
- Replaced internal disturbance with armed rebellion as a ground for National Emergency under Article 352.
- Required written advice of the Union Cabinet for proclaiming National Emergency.
- Protected Articles 20 and 21 from suspension of enforcement even during National Emergency.
- Provided additional safeguards concerning Emergency proclamations.

Prelims points

- The Right to Property is a constitutional/legal right, not a Fundamental Right.
- Article 300A states that no person shall be deprived of property except by authority of law.
- The grounds for National Emergency are war, external aggression and armed rebellion.
- Articles 20 and 21 retain special protection during Emergency.

## 3. Important amendments: 1985–2002

### 3.1 Fifty-Second Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985

Major provisions

- Introduced the Anti-Defection Law.
- Added the Tenth Schedule.
- Provided for disqualification of legislators on specified grounds of defection.

Prelims points

- The Tenth Schedule applies to members of Parliament and State Legislatures.
- The Speaker or Chairman ordinarily decides questions of disqualification under the Tenth Schedule, subject to judicial review.
- The earlier protection for a split by one-third of members was removed by the 91st Amendment.

### 3.2 Sixty-First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1988

Major provision

- Reduced the voting age for elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies from 21 years to 18 years.
- Amended Article 326.

Prelims trap: The 61st Amendment lowered the voting age, not the minimum age for contesting elections.

### 3.3 Sixty-Ninth Constitutional Amendment Act, 1991

Major provisions

- Inserted Articles 239AA and 239AB.
- Provided a Legislative Assembly and Council of Ministers for the National Capital Territory of Delhi.
- Established Delhi's special constitutional arrangement.

Prelims points

- Delhi is a Union Territory with a legislature, not a full-fledged State.
- Public order, police and land are excluded from the Delhi Legislative Assembly's legislative competence under Article 239AA.

### 3.4 Seventy-First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

Major provision

\- Added Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali to the Eighth Schedule.

### 3.5 Seventy-Third Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

Major provisions

- Granted constitutional status to Panchayati Raj Institutions.
- Inserted Part IX — Articles 243 to 243O.
- Added the Eleventh Schedule, containing 29 subjects.
- Provided for a three-tier Panchayati Raj structure, subject to constitutional exceptions.
- Provided for regular elections, reservations and State Election Commissions.
- Provided for the constitution of State Finance Commissions.

Prelims points

- Came into force on 24 April 1993.
- Gram Sabha is constitutionally recognised.
- At least one-third of seats are reserved for women, including the relevant reserved-category seats.
- A Panchayat's normal duration is five years.
- The State Election Commission conducts Panchayat elections.

### 3.6 Seventy-Fourth Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

Major provisions

- Granted constitutional status to Municipalities.
- Inserted Part IXA — Articles 243P to 243ZG.
- Added the Twelfth Schedule, containing 18 subjects.
- Recognised three broad categories of urban local bodies:
  - Nagar Panchayat
  - Municipal Council
  - Municipal Corporation
- Provided for reservations, municipal elections and financial arrangements.
- Provided for District Planning Committees and Metropolitan Planning Committees.

Prelims points

- Came into force on 1 June 1993.
- Municipal elections are supervised by the State Election Commission, not the Election Commission of India.
- The State Finance Commission reviews the financial position of Panchayats and Municipalities.

### 3.7 Seventy-Sixth Constitutional Amendment Act, 1994

Major provision

\- Placed the Tamil Nadu law providing 69% reservation in educational institutions and public employment in the Ninth Schedule.

Prelims distinction: Inclusion in the Ninth Schedule does not create absolute immunity from basic-structure judicial review.

### 3.8 Eighty-Sixth Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002

Major provisions

1. Inserted Article 21A, making free and compulsory education a Fundamental Right for children aged 6–14 years.
2. Modified Article 45 to concern early childhood care and education for children below six years.
3. Added Article 51A(k), imposing a Fundamental Duty on parents or guardians to provide opportunities for education to children aged 6–14 years.

Prelims points

- Article 21A applies to children aged 6–14, not 0–14.
- Article 45 is a Directive Principle.
- Article 51A(k) is a Fundamental Duty.
- The Right to Education Act, 2009, operationalised Article 21A from 1 April 2010.

## 4. Important amendments: 2003–2014

### 4.1 Eighty-Ninth Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003

Major provisions

- Separated the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes from the National Commission for Scheduled Castes.
- Provided for the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST) under Article 338A.
- The NCSC continues under Article 338.

### 4.2 Ninety-First Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003

Major provisions

- Limited the size of the Union Council of Ministers to 15% of the total membership of the Lok Sabha, including the Prime Minister.
- Limited the size of the State Council of Ministers to 15% of the total membership of the Legislative Assembly, including the Chief Minister.
- Prescribed a minimum of 12 ministers, including the Chief Minister, in a State.
- Removed the one-third split exception under the Anti-Defection Law.
- Introduced restrictions on ministerial appointments for members disqualified for defection.

Prelims points

- The 15% ceiling for State ministers is calculated on Assembly membership, not the combined membership of both Houses.
- There is no corresponding constitutional minimum of 12 ministers at the Union level.
- The Tenth Schedule still recognises a qualifying merger involving at least two-thirds of the members of a legislature party.

### 4.3 Ninety-Second Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003

Major provision

Added four languages to the Eighth Schedule:

- Bodo
- Dogri
- Maithili
- Santhali

Prelims point: The Eighth Schedule presently contains 22 languages.

### 4.4 Ninety-Third Constitutional Amendment Act, 2005

Major provision

- Inserted Article 15(5).
- Enabled special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes and SCs/STs concerning admission to educational institutions, including certain private institutions.
- Excluded minority educational institutions covered by Article 30(1).

Prelims distinction: Article 15(5) concerns admissions to educational institutions, not reservations in government employment.

### 4.5 Ninety-Seventh Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011

Major provisions

- Added the words co-operative societies to Article 19(1)(c).
- Inserted Article 43B, a Directive Principle concerning promotion of co-operative societies.
- Added Part IXB, dealing with co-operative societies.

Important judicial development

In Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021), the Supreme Court held that the 97th Amendment's Part IXB provisions were invalid insofar as they applied to co-operative societies operating exclusively within a State, because the necessary State ratification had not been obtained.

Part IXB continues to operate in relation to multi-State co-operative societies.

Prelims trap: The entire 97th Amendment was not struck down.

### 4.6 Ninety-Ninth Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014

Major provision

\- Sought to establish the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) for appointments to the Supreme Court and High Courts.

Judicial development

- In 2015, the Supreme Court declared the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act unconstitutional.
- The collegium system therefore continued.

Prelims point: The NJAC is not the currently operative constitutional mechanism for appointing Supreme Court and High Court judges.

### 5.1 One Hundredth Constitutional Amendment Act, 2015

Major provision

- Gave constitutional effect to the India–Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement.
- Facilitated exchange of enclaves and settlement of outstanding boundary issues.
- Amended the First Schedule to reflect territorial changes.

Prelims point: The 100th Amendment concerns territorial arrangements between India and Bangladesh, not GST.

### 5.2 One Hundred and First Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016

Major provisions

- Introduced the constitutional framework for the Goods and Services Tax (GST).
- Inserted Article 246A, providing special legislative powers concerning GST.
- Inserted Article 269A, concerning GST on inter-State supplies.
- Inserted Article 279A, providing for the GST Council.
- Omitted Article 268A and modified several other constitutional provisions.

#### GST Council — Article 279A

| Position    | Membership                                                                |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Chairperson | Union Finance Minister                                                    |
| Member      | Union Minister of State in charge of Revenue or Finance                   |
| Members     | State Finance Ministers or other ministers nominated by State Governments |

Voting provisions

- Decisions require at least three-fourths of the weighted votes of members present and voting.
- Central Government's vote has a weight of one-third.
- All State Governments together have a weight of two-thirds.

Prelims points

- GST is a destination-based indirect tax.
- Parliament and State Legislatures generally have concurrent legislative powers over GST under Article 246A.
- Parliament has exclusive power over GST on supplies in the course of inter-State trade or commerce under Article 246A(2).
- In Union of India v. Mohit Minerals (2022), the Supreme Court clarified that GST Council recommendations are not legally binding on Parliament and State Legislatures.

### 5.3 One Hundred and Second Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018

Major provisions

- Granted constitutional status to the National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC).
- Inserted Article 338B.
- Inserted Article 342A, concerning identification of socially and educationally backward classes.
- Added a definition of socially and educationally backward classes in Article 366.

Prelims distinction

| Commission                               | Constitutional article |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| National Commission for Scheduled Castes | 338                    |
| National Commission for Scheduled Tribes | 338A                   |
| National Commission for Backward Classes | 338B                   |

### 5.4 One Hundred and Third Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019

Major provisions

- Introduced provisions for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS).
- Inserted Articles 15(6) and 16(6).
- Enabled reservation of up to 10% for EWS, subject to constitutional and statutory conditions.

Prelims points

- Article 15(6) concerns educational institutions, including eligible private institutions, but excludes minority educational institutions under Article 30(1).
- Article 16(6) concerns appointments or posts under the State.
- The EWS provisions exclude classes already covered by the specified SC/ST/OBC reservation framework.
- The Supreme Court upheld the 103rd Amendment in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022), by a 3:2 majority.

### 5.5 One Hundred and Fourth Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019

Major provisions

- Amended Article 334.
- Extended reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies for another ten years, until 25 January 2030.
- Did not extend the special representation of the Anglo-Indian community through nomination.

Prelims points

- The Anglo-Indian nomination provisions previously concerned the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies, not the Rajya Sabha.
- The SC/ST seat reservation continues under Article 334.
- The constitutional provision for presidential nomination of 12 Rajya Sabha members under Article 80 is entirely different and continues.

### 5.6 One Hundred and Fifth Constitutional Amendment Act, 2021

Major provisions

- Restored and expressly clarified the power of States and Union Territories to identify socially and educationally backward classes for their own purposes.
- Amended Articles 338B, 342A and 366.

Prelims distinction

- The Central List of socially and educationally backward classes is governed by the constitutional framework of Article 342A.
- States and Union Territories may maintain their own lists for their respective purposes.

The 105th Amendment followed the Supreme Court's interpretation of the 102nd Amendment in the Maratha reservation case.

### 5.7 One Hundred and Sixth Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023

Popular name: Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam

Major provisions

- Provides for reservation of approximately one-third of seats for women in:
  - Lok Sabha
  - State Legislative Assemblies
  - Legislative Assembly of the National Capital Territory of Delhi
- Includes reservation for women within seats reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
- Inserted Articles 330A, 332A and 334A and amended Article 239AA.

#### When will women's reservation become operational?

The amendment provides that reservation will take effect after:

1. Publication of relevant figures from the first Census conducted after commencement of the amendment; and
2. A delimitation exercise undertaken for this purpose.

The reservation is therefore not automatically operational merely because the Amendment Act was enacted.

Article 334A also provides for a 15-year duration from the commencement of the reservation provision, subject to continuation as determined by Parliament by law.

Prelims points

- The 106th Amendment does not itself provide one-third reservation in the Rajya Sabha or State Legislative Councils.
- The provision includes SC/ST reserved seats.
- It does not establish a separate reservation quota for OBC women.
- Its implementation is linked to Census figures and delimitation.

### Important clarification on delimitation

The 84th Amendment continued the freeze on the allocation of Lok Sabha seats among States and the total seats in State Assemblies until after the first Census following 2026.

Therefore, freezing the number of seats and redrawing constituency boundaries are not the same thing.

### Special note: Article 31C and the 2024 judgment

The 2024 Supreme Court judgment clarified two important points:

- Article 31C continues to protect qualifying laws implementing Article 39(b) and Article 39(c) from challenges under Articles 14 and 19.
- Not every privately owned resource automatically qualifies as a material resource of the community under Article 39(b); the nature and characteristics of the resource matter.

The Court confirmed that the original protection under Article 31C survived the invalidation of the 42nd Amendment's attempted expansion.&#x20;

## 9. High-priority UPSC Prelims traps

1. 1st Amendment: Introduced the Ninth Schedule, not the Tenth Schedule.
2. 42nd Amendment: Introduced Fundamental Duties; the original Constitution did not contain them.
3. 44th Amendment: Removed the Right to Property from Fundamental Rights, but did not abolish constitutional protection of property.
4. 52nd Amendment: Introduced the Anti-Defection Law.
5. 61st Amendment: Reduced voting age, not candidature age.
6. 73rd and 74th Amendments: Concern Panchayats and Municipalities respectively.
7. 86th Amendment: Article 21A covers ages 6–14, while Article 45 concerns children below six.
8. 91st Amendment: Ministry-size ceiling includes the Prime Minister or Chief Minister, as applicable.
9. 97th Amendment: Was only partially invalidated by the Supreme Court.
10. 99th Amendment: The NJAC was declared unconstitutional.
11. 101st Amendment: Established the GST constitutional framework.
12. 102nd Amendment: Granted constitutional status to NCBC.
13. 103rd Amendment: Introduced EWS reservation.
14. 104th Amendment: Extended SC/ST legislative reservations but not Anglo-Indian nomination.
15. 105th Amendment: Restored and clarified State/UT powers regarding their own SEBC lists.
16. 106th Amendment: Women's legislative reservation is subject to its constitutional implementation conditions.
17. Ninth Schedule: Inclusion does not guarantee immunity from basic-structure review.
18. Article 368: No joint sitting for Constitutional Amendment Bills.
19. State ratification: Not necessary for every Constitutional Amendment.
20. Basic Structure Doctrine: Parliament's constitutional amendment power is extensive but not unlimited.

## Section A — Foundational and Historical Amendments

### Q1. Consider the following statements about the First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951:

1. It inserted Article 15(4).
2. It introduced the Ninth Schedule.
3. It inserted the Tenth Schedule to prevent political defections.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

<div>
<strong>A.</strong> 1 and 2 only<br>
<strong>B.</strong> 2 and 3 only<br>
<strong>C.</strong> 1 and 3 only<br>
<strong>D.</strong> 1, 2 and 3
</div>

**Answer: A**

> **Explanation:** The First Amendment inserted Article 15(4), Articles 31A–31B and the Ninth Schedule. The Tenth Schedule was introduced by the 52nd Amendment in 1985.
