# Constitutional Provisions related to Budget, Money and Financial Bills

## Article 110 — Definition of Money Bill

Under Article 110(1), a Bill is a Money Bill only if it contains provisions dealing exclusively with one or more of the matters specified in clauses (a) to (g).

### 1. Matters covered under Article 110(1)

| Clause | Subject matter                                                                                                                             |
| ------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| (a)    | Imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of any tax                                                                      |
| (b)    | Borrowing of money, government guarantees and financial obligations of the Government of India                                             |
| (c)    | Custody of the Consolidated Fund of India or Contingency Fund of India; payment into or withdrawal from these Funds                        |
| (d)    | Appropriation (authorisation of expenditure) of money from the Consolidated Fund of India                                                  |
| (e)    | Declaration of expenditure as charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, or increase in such expenditure                                   |
| (f)    | Receipt, custody or issue of money relating to the Consolidated Fund of India or Public Account of India; audit of Union or State accounts |
| (g)    | Matters incidental to any of the above matters                                                                                             |

### 2. Matters that do not automatically make a Bill a Money Bill — Article 110(2)

A Bill is not considered a Money Bill merely because it provides for:

1. Imposition of fines or other monetary penalties.
2. Collection of licence fees.
3. Collection of fees for services rendered.
4. Imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of taxes by local authorities for local purposes.

Important distinction: A Bill dealing with a Union tax may qualify under Article 110(1)(a), whereas a Bill dealing only with local-authority taxation does not qualify merely on that ground.

### 3. Certification of Money Bills — Article 110(3) and (4)

- Article 110(3): The Speaker of the Lok Sabha decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill. The Constitution states that the Speaker's decision shall be final.
- Article 110(4): The Speaker's signed certificate must be endorsed on every Money Bill when:
  - It is transmitted to the Rajya Sabha under Article 109.
  - It is presented to the President for assent under Article 111.

Judicial review: The constitutional finality of the Speaker's decision does not create absolute immunity from judicial review. Money Bill certification can be examined by courts on constitutional grounds.

### 4. Important distinctions for UPSC Prelims

- A Bill containing a provision related to taxation is not necessarily a Money Bill. It must satisfy the exclusive-subject requirement of Article 110(1).
- A Bill containing provisions unrelated to Article 110(1)(a)–(g) cannot qualify merely because it also contains taxation provisions.
- The Public Account of India is expressly mentioned in clause (f), but not in clause (c).
- Audit of accounts of both the Union and States is covered under clause (f).
- The Speaker of the Lok Sabha, not the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, certifies Money Bills.
- Article 110 defines a Money Bill; Article 109 prescribes its special legislative procedure.
- The corresponding provision for Money Bills in State Legislatures is Article 199, where the Speaker of the Legislative Assembly performs the certification function.

Most important Prelims trap: Not every Financial Bill is a Money Bill. A Money Bill must satisfy the specific conditions of Article 110(1).

## Article 117 — Special Provisions as to Financial Bills

Article 117 specifies the requirements relating to President's recommendation, introduction and passage of Financial Bills in Parliament.

### 1. Article 117(1) — Financial Bills of Category A

A Bill containing provisions relating to any of the matters specified in Article 110(1)(a) to (f), along with other matters not covered under Article 110(1), is commonly called a Financial Bill of Category A.

- Such a Bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha.
- President's recommendation is required for its introduction.
- An amendment making provision for matters under Article 110(1)(a)–(f) also requires the President's recommendation.
- Exception: No recommendation is required for moving an amendment proposing the reduction or abolition of any tax.
- Unlike a Money Bill, a Financial Bill of Category A can be amended or rejected by the Rajya Sabha.
- A joint sitting is possible in case of a legislative deadlock.

### 2. Article 117(2) — Matters that do not attract Article 117(1) merely by themselves

A Bill or amendment does not attract the special requirements of Article 117(1) merely because it provides for:

1. Imposition of fines or monetary penalties.
2. Collection of licence fees.
3. Collection of fees for services rendered.
4. Imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of taxes by local authorities for local purposes.

These exclusions correspond to those specified in Article 110(2).

### 3. Article 117(3) — Financial Bills of Category B

A Bill that, if enacted and brought into operation, would involve expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India is commonly called a Financial Bill of Category B, provided it does not fall within the Money Bill or Category A classification.

- Such a Bill can be introduced in either House of Parliament.
- President's recommendation is not required for introduction merely under Article 117(3).
- However, it cannot be passed by either House unless the President has recommended its consideration to that House.
- Both Houses have equal legislative powers.
- A joint sitting is possible in case of a deadlock.

### 4. Comparison — Money Bill and Financial Bills

| Feature                            | Money Bill                        | Financial Bill (A)                               | Financial Bill (B)                                                           |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Constitutional provision           | Article 110                       | Article 117(1)                                   | Article 117(3)                                                               |
| Nature                             | Only matters under Article 110(1) | Article 110(1)(a)–(f) matters plus other matters | Involves expenditure from CFI, without falling into the first two categories |
| Introduction                       | Lok Sabha only                    | Lok Sabha only                                   | Either House                                                                 |
| President's recommendation         | Required for introduction         | Required for introduction                        | Required before passage, for consideration by each House                     |
| Speaker's Money Bill certification | Required                          | Not required                                     | Not required                                                                 |
| Rajya Sabha's powers               | Can only recommend changes        | Can amend or reject                              | Can amend or reject                                                          |
| Joint sitting                      | Not permitted                     | Permitted                                        | Permitted                                                                    |

### 5. Important Prelims Distinctions

- Article 117(1) refers specifically to clauses (a) to (f) of Article 110(1), not clause (g).
- Article 117(1) expressly exempts amendments proposing the reduction or abolition of any tax from its recommendation requirement.
- Article 117(3) concerns expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India, not merely receipt of money into the Fund.
- A Financial Bill of Category B may be introduced without prior presidential recommendation under Article 117(3), but cannot be passed without the required recommendation.
- The requirement of presidential recommendation under Article 117(3) applies to both Houses.
- All Money Bills are Financial Bills in the broad sense, but not all Financial Bills are Money Bills.
- Financial Bills of Categories A and B follow the ordinary legislative procedure, subject to their special constitutional requirements.

Most important Prelims trap: Under Article 117(1), presidential recommendation is required at the introduction stage, whereas under Article 117(3), the special requirement operates before passage by either House.

## Examples of Financial Bills — Category A and Category B

### 1. Financial Bill Category A — Article 117(1)

Example: Suppose Parliament proposes a Bill containing two provisions:

1. Imposition of a new Union tax on certain goods.
2. Establishment of a regulatory authority to supervise the production and sale of those goods.

Why is it Category A?

- Provision 1 deals with taxation under Article 110(1)(a).
- Provision 2 deals with the establishment and functioning of a regulatory authority, which is not, by itself, a Money Bill matter.
- Since the Bill combines a Money Bill matter with an unrelated legislative matter, it is a Financial Bill Category A, not a Money Bill.

Constitutional requirements:

- Introduced only in Lok Sabha.
- President's recommendation required for introduction.
- Rajya Sabha can amend or reject it.

### 2. Financial Bill Category B — Article 117(3)

Example: Suppose Parliament proposes a Bill to establish a new Central University.

The Bill provides for:

1. Establishment of the university.
2. Appointment of its Vice-Chancellor and other officials.
3. Academic administration and regulation of courses.
4. Payment of salaries and operational expenses from the Consolidated Fund of India.

Why is it Category B?

- Its principal subject is the establishment and administration of a university.
- It does not contain provisions concerning the specific Money Bill matters under Article 110(1).
- However, implementing the Bill would involve expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India.

Therefore, it is a Financial Bill Category B.

Constitutional requirements:

- Can be introduced in either House.
- President's recommendation is required before either House passes it.
- Rajya Sabha can amend or reject it.

### 3. Quick Comparison

| Bill                                                                                       | Classification            | Reason                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Bill dealing exclusively with imposition of a Union tax                                    | Money Bill                | Only Article 110 matter                          |
| Bill imposing a Union tax and establishing an unrelated regulatory authority               | Financial Bill Category A | Article 110 matter + other matters               |
| Bill establishing a Central University and providing for expenditure from CFI              | Financial Bill Category B | Expenditure from CFI without Article 110 matters |
| Bill regulating universities without involving expenditure from CFI or Article 110 matters | Ordinary Bill             | Neither condition applies                        |

Remember for UPSC Prelims:

- Category A: Contains an Article 110(1)(a)–(f) matter plus other legislative matters.
- Category B: Does not contain Money Bill matters but would involve expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India.

These are illustrative hypothetical Bills, not names of specific enacted laws.

## Article 112 — Annual Financial Statement

### 1. Article 112(1) — Annual Financial Statement

- The Annual Financial Statement (AFS) is the constitutional name for the Union Budget.
- The President causes it to be laid before both Houses of Parliament for every financial year.
- It contains the estimated receipts and expenditure of the Government of India for that financial year.
- The financial year runs from 1 April to 31 March.
- The Constitution uses the expression Annual Financial Statement, not Budget.

### 2. Article 112(2) — Classification of Expenditure

The Annual Financial Statement must separately show:

1. Charged expenditure: Expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, which is not submitted to Parliament for voting.
2. Voted expenditure: Other expenditure proposed from the Consolidated Fund of India, which is submitted to the Lok Sabha for voting through Demands for Grants.

It must also distinguish revenue expenditure from other expenditure (capital expenditure).

### 3. Article 112(3) — Expenditure Charged on the Consolidated Fund of India

The following expenditures are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India:

| Clause   | Charged expenditure                                                                                                                                                       |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| (a)      | Emoluments and allowances of the President and other expenditure relating to the President's office                                                                       |
| (b)      | Salaries and allowances of the Chairman and Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha and Speaker and Deputy Speaker of Lok Sabha                                                    |
| (c)      | Debt charges of the Government of India, including interest, sinking fund charges, redemption charges and expenditure relating to government borrowing and debt servicing |
| (d)(i)   | Salaries, allowances and pensions of Supreme Court judges                                                                                                                 |
| (d)(ii)  | Pensions of Federal Court judges                                                                                                                                          |
| (d)(iii) | Pensions of qualifying High Court judges                                                                                                                                  |
| (e)      | Salary, allowances and pension of the Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG)                                                                                      |
| (f)      | Sums required to satisfy judgments, decrees or awards of courts or arbitral tribunals                                                                                     |
| (g)      | Any other expenditure declared charged by the Constitution or by Parliament through law                                                                                   |

### 4. Other Important Charged Expenditures

In addition to the items expressly listed in Article 112(3), the Constitution provides for certain other charged expenditures.

| Constitutional provision | Expenditure                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Article 146(3)           | Administrative expenses of the Supreme Court, including salaries, allowances and pensions of its officers and servants       |
| Article 148(6)           | Administrative expenses of the office of the CAG, including salaries, allowances and pensions of its staff                   |
| Article 322              | Expenses of the Union Public Service Commission (UPSC), including salaries, allowances and pensions of its members and staff |
| Article 273              | Grants in lieu of export duty on jute and jute products, as constitutionally provided                                        |

Important distinction: The salaries, allowances and pensions of Supreme Court judges are charged on the CFI, whereas, for High Court judges, Article 112(3)(d)(iii) specifically covers their pensions. Salaries and allowances of High Court judges are charged on the Consolidated Fund of the respective State under Article 202(3)(d).

### 5. Charged Expenditure vs Voted Expenditure

| Feature                  | Charged expenditure        | Voted expenditure          |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Fund                     | Consolidated Fund of India | Consolidated Fund of India |
| Voting in Lok Sabha      | Not subject to voting      | Subject to voting          |
| Discussion in Parliament | Permitted                  | Permitted                  |
| Demand for Grants        | Not submitted for voting   | Submitted to Lok Sabha     |
| Appropriation Act        | Included                   | Included                   |

Important: Charged expenditure is not subject to voting, but it can be discussed in Parliament. It also requires appropriation through law before withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund of India.

### 6. High-Yield Prelims Distinctions

- The President causes the Annual Financial Statement to be laid before Parliament; the Finance Minister ordinarily presents the Union Budget.
- The Annual Financial Statement is laid before both Houses, but Demands for Grants are voted on only by the Lok Sabha.
- Charged expenditure is not voted upon, but Parliament can discuss it.
- Both charged and voted expenditures are included in the Appropriation Bill.
- Salaries and allowances of the Speaker and Deputy Speaker of Lok Sabha are charged expenditures.
- Salaries and allowances of the Chairman and Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha are charged expenditures.
- The Vice-President's salary as Chairman of Rajya Sabha is charged on the CFI; the Constitution does not provide a separate salary merely for holding the office of Vice-President.
- UPSC expenses are charged on the CFI under Article 322.
- Election Commission expenses are not automatically charged on the CFI merely because it is a constitutional body.
- Charged expenditure is not necessarily capital expenditure. Charged/voted and revenue/capital are different classifications.

Most important Prelims trap: Charged expenditure is exempt from voting in the Lok Sabha, not from parliamentary discussion or the requirement of appropriation by law.

# Parliament

## 1. Constitutional framework of Parliament

### 1.1 Composition of Parliament

Article 79 provides that the Parliament of India consists of three components:

1. The President of India
2. The Council of States (Rajya Sabha)
3. The House of the People (Lok Sabha)

The President is an integral part of Parliament but is not a member of either House.

The President's parliamentary functions include summoning and proroguing Parliament, dissolving the Lok Sabha, addressing Parliament, giving assent to Bills and promulgating Ordinances under Article 123.

## 2. Rajya Sabha — Council of States

### 2.1 Composition

Under Article 80:

- Maximum strength: 250 members.
- Maximum elected representatives of States and Union Territories: 238.
- Nominated members: 12.
- Present sanctioned strength: 245, comprising 233 elected and 12 nominated members.

The President nominates 12 members having special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art and social service.

### 2.2 Election of Rajya Sabha members

- Representatives of States are elected by the elected members of State Legislative Assemblies.
- Election follows proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote (STV).
- Voting is conducted through an open ballot, subject to the applicable election rules.
- Seats are allocated to States mainly according to population, as specified in the Fourth Schedule.
- Representation of Union Territories is determined by parliamentary law.

Important distinction: Rajya Sabha elections use an open-ballot system, whereas presidential and vice-presidential elections use secret ballots.

### 2.3 Duration

- Rajya Sabha is a permanent House and is not subject to dissolution.
- Each member normally serves six years.
- Approximately one-third of members retire every two years.
- A member elected to fill a casual vacancy serves only the remainder of the predecessor's term.

### 2.4 Presiding officers

- Vice-President of India: Ex officio Chairman of Rajya Sabha.
- Deputy Chairman: Elected by Rajya Sabha from among its members.
- The Chairman is not a member of Rajya Sabha.
- The Deputy Chairman is a member of Rajya Sabha.

## 3. Lok Sabha — House of the People

### 3.1 Composition

Under Article 81, the constitutional maximum for elected representation is:

- 530 members representing States.
- 20 members representing Union Territories.

The current elected strength is 543 seats, subject to vacancies.

Members are directly elected through universal adult franchise using the first-past-the-post electoral system.

### 3.2 Anglo-Indian representation

Previously, Article 331 empowered the President to nominate up to two members of the Anglo-Indian community to the Lok Sabha if the community was considered inadequately represented.

This special representation ceased in January 2020 because the 104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019 did not extend the relevant 70-year period under Article 334.

Consequently:

- There is currently no operative provision for Anglo-Indian nomination to the Lok Sabha.
- The nomination of 12 members to Rajya Sabha under Article 80 continues.
- Reservation of Lok Sabha and State Assembly seats for SCs and STs has been extended until 2030.

### 3.3 Duration of Lok Sabha

Under Article 83(2):

- Normal term: Five years from the date appointed for its first meeting.
- The President may dissolve Lok Sabha before the expiry of its term.
- During a National Emergency, Parliament may extend its term by law for one year at a time.
- Such an extension cannot continue beyond six months after the Emergency ceases.

### 3.4 Speaker and Deputy Speaker

Under Article 93, Lok Sabha chooses two members as its Speaker and Deputy Speaker.

Speaker:

- Presides over Lok Sabha sittings.
- Maintains order and regulates proceedings.
- Decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill under Article 110.
- Presides over a joint sitting under Article 108.
- Decides disqualification petitions under the Tenth Schedule, subject to judicial review.
- Exercises a casting vote in case of equality of votes.

The Speaker ordinarily does not vote in the first instance.

Deputy Speaker:

- Performs the Speaker's duties when the Speaker's office is vacant or when the Speaker is absent.
- When presiding, exercises the powers of the Speaker.
- When not presiding, may vote as an ordinary member.

### 3.5 Removal and continuity of Speaker

The Speaker or Deputy Speaker may be removed by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of Lok Sabha, with at least 14 days' notice.

The Speaker continues in office after dissolution of Lok Sabha until immediately before the first meeting of the new Lok Sabha. This special continuity does not extend to the Deputy Speaker.

## 4. Qualifications and disqualifications

### 4.1 Qualifications under Article 84

A person must:

- Be a citizen of India.
- Make the prescribed oath or affirmation before the person authorised by the Election Commission.
- Be at least 30 years old for Rajya Sabha.
- Be at least 25 years old for Lok Sabha.
- Possess other qualifications prescribed by Parliament.

The age qualification is different from the minimum age for voting, which is 18 years.

### 4.2 Disqualifications under Article 102

A person is disqualified for membership if:

- Holding an office of profit under the Union or State government, except an exempted office.
- Being of unsound mind and so declared by a competent court.
- Being an undischarged insolvent.
- Not being an Indian citizen, or voluntarily acquiring foreign citizenship, or acknowledging allegiance to a foreign State.
- Being disqualified under a law made by Parliament.

Additional disqualifications are prescribed under the Representation of the People Act, 1951.

### 4.3 Anti-defection law

The Tenth Schedule was inserted by the 52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985 and modified by the 91st Amendment Act, 2003.

A member may be disqualified for:

- Voluntarily giving up membership of the political party.
- Voting or abstaining contrary to the party's direction without prior permission, unless condoned within 15 days.
- An independent member joining a political party after election.
- A nominated member joining a political party after six months from taking their seat.

Merger exception: Disqualification may be avoided when at least two-thirds of the legislature party agree to a merger, subject to the Tenth Schedule's requirements.

The former one-third split exception was deleted by the 91st Amendment.

The Speaker or Chairman decides disqualification petitions, subject to judicial review.

### 4.4 Authority to decide disqualification of Members of Parliament

The authority competent to decide the disqualification of an MP depends on the ground of disqualification.

| Ground of disqualification                   | Deciding authority                                                                  | Constitutional provision    |
| -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Defection                                    | Speaker of Lok Sabha or Chairman of Rajya Sabha, as applicable                      | Tenth Schedule, Paragraph 6 |
| Other disqualifications under Article 102(1) | President of India, after obtaining the opinion of the Election Commission of India | Article 103                 |

#### A. Disqualification on grounds of defection

Under the Tenth Schedule:

- For Lok Sabha members, the Speaker of Lok Sabha decides disqualification petitions.
- For Rajya Sabha members, the Chairman of Rajya Sabha decides disqualification petitions.
- If the disqualification petition concerns the Speaker or Chairman, the decision is made by a member of the respective House elected by that House for this purpose.
- Decisions under the Tenth Schedule are subject to judicial review.

In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992), the Supreme Court upheld the anti-defection framework while allowing judicial review of decisions of the presiding officer.

#### B. Disqualification on other grounds

Under Articles 102(1) and 103, disqualification questions involving grounds such as office of profit, undischarged insolvency, loss of citizenship or disqualification under parliamentary law are decided by the President.

The procedure is:

1. A question arises as to whether a sitting MP has become subject to a disqualification under Article 102(1).
2. The question is referred to the President.
3. The President obtains the opinion of the Election Commission of India.
4. The President must act according to the Election Commission's opinion.

The President does not exercise independent discretion to disregard the Election Commission's opinion.

Important UPSC distinctions:

- Defection: Speaker/Chairman decides, not the President.
- Other disqualifications under Article 102(1): President decides, bound by the Election Commission's opinion.
- Election disputes: The High Court adjudicates election petitions under the Representation of the People Act, 1951, with appeals to the Supreme Court. This is distinct from the Article 103 procedure.
- Judicial review: Decisions on disqualification are subject to judicial scrutiny.

## 5. Parliamentary sessions and proceedings

### 5.1 Sessions

Under Article 85, the President summons each House.

The gap between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next session cannot exceed six months.

The Constitution does not prescribe three sessions annually, although Parliament ordinarily meets in:

- Budget Session
- Monsoon Session
- Winter Session

### 5.2 Adjournment, prorogation and dissolution

| Feature               | Adjournment        | Prorogation    | Dissolution                |
| --------------------- | ------------------ | -------------- | -------------------------- |
| Meaning               | Suspends a sitting | Ends a session | Ends the life of Lok Sabha |
| Authority             | Presiding officer  | President      | President                  |
| Applies to            | Both Houses        | Both Houses    | Lok Sabha only             |
| Terminates membership | No                 | No             | Yes, for Lok Sabha         |

Adjournment sine die means adjournment without fixing a date for the next sitting. It does not itself amount to prorogation.

### 5.3 Quorum

Under Article 100(3), quorum is one-tenth of the total number of members of the House.

- Lok Sabha, with 543 seats: 55 members.
- Rajya Sabha, with 245 seats: 25 members.

These figures include the presiding officer where applicable and assume the stated total strengths.

## 7. Powers of the two Houses

### 7.1 Areas of equal power

Both Houses generally have equal powers in:

- Ordinary legislation.
- Constitutional amendment Bills.
- Impeachment of the President.
- Removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges.
- Approval of Emergency proclamations, subject to constitutional provisions.
- Election of the Vice-President.

### 7.2 Special powers of Lok Sabha

- Money Bills can be introduced only in Lok Sabha.
- The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible only to Lok Sabha.
- No-confidence motions are moved only in Lok Sabha.
- Demands for Grants are voted upon only in Lok Sabha.
- In a joint sitting, Lok Sabha generally has a numerical advantage.

### 7.3 Special powers of Rajya Sabha

Article 249: Rajya Sabha may authorise Parliament to legislate on a State List matter in the national interest by a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.

The resolution operates for a period not exceeding one year and may be renewed.

Article 312: Rajya Sabha may authorise the creation of one or more All-India Services by a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.

Article 67(b): A resolution to remove the Vice-President must originate in Rajya Sabha and requires a majority of all its then members, followed by a simple majority in the Lok Sabha.

## 8. Legislative procedure

### 8.1 Ordinary Bills

An ordinary Bill may originate in either House and may be introduced by a minister or private member.

It must be passed by both Houses in identical form and receive the President's assent to become law.

The President may assent, withhold assent or return an ordinary Bill for reconsideration. If Parliament passes the returned Bill again, with or without amendments, the President cannot withhold assent.

### 8.2 Money Bills

Under Article 110, a Money Bill must contain only provisions dealing with matters specified in Article 110, including taxation, borrowing, the Consolidated Fund of India, appropriation and certain related financial matters.

- Introduced only in Lok Sabha.
- Requires the President's recommendation for introduction.
- Rajya Sabha cannot amend or reject it.
- Rajya Sabha may recommend amendments within 14 days.
- Lok Sabha may accept or reject those recommendations.
- If Rajya Sabha does not return it within 14 days, the Bill is deemed passed by both Houses in the form passed by Lok Sabha.

The Speaker certifies a Money Bill. The certification is constitutionally significant but not completely immune from judicial review.

The President cannot return a Money Bill for reconsideration under Article 111.

### 8.3 Financial Bills

| Feature                      | Money Bill             | Financial Bill under Article 117(1) | Financial Bill under Article 117(3) |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Contains Article 110 matters | Exclusively            | Along with other matters            | May not contain Article 110 matters |
| Introduced in                | Lok Sabha only         | Lok Sabha only                      | Either House                        |
| President's recommendation   | For introduction       | For introduction                    | Before consideration for passage    |
| Rajya Sabha's power          | Recommend changes only | Ordinary legislative powers         | Ordinary legislative powers         |
| Joint sitting                | No                     | Possible                            | Possible                            |

### 8.4 Constitutional amendment Bills

Under Article 368:

- May originate in either House.
- May be introduced by a minister or private member.
- Do not require the President's prior recommendation.
- Require the prescribed special majority in each House.
- Certain amendments additionally require ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures.
- There is no joint sitting to resolve a deadlock.
- The President must give assent to a duly passed constitutional amendment Bill.

### 8.5 Joint sitting

Under Article 108, a joint sitting may be summoned when:

1. One House rejects a Bill passed by the other.
2. The Houses finally disagree on amendments.
3. More than six months pass without the other House passing the Bill, subject to the constitutional calculation of time.

A joint sitting is unavailable for Money Bills and constitutional amendment Bills.

The Speaker of Lok Sabha ordinarily presides.

Your reasoning is understandable. However, rejection by one House does not necessarily mean that the Bill has been finally disposed of by Parliament.

The distinction is between rejection by one House and final failure of the Bill in Parliament.

### Example

Suppose an Ordinary Bill is introduced in Lok Sabha.

1. Lok Sabha passes the Bill and sends it to Rajya Sabha.
2. Rajya Sabha rejects the Bill.
3. The Bill has now been passed by one House but rejected by the other.
4. Therefore, the two Houses have failed to agree on the Bill. This constitutes a deadlock under Article 108.
5. The President may summon a joint sitting to resolve the disagreement.

At the joint sitting:

- If the required majority supports the Bill, it is deemed passed by both Houses.
- If the required majority does not support the Bill, it fails.

### 8.6 Deadlock

Because, for an Ordinary Bill, neither House has an absolute veto over the other House's approval.

If rejection by either House automatically ended the legislative process, there would be no mechanism to resolve disagreements between the Houses.

Article 108 provides that mechanism through a joint sitting.

### Important distinction

| Situation                                           | Result                                            |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Lok Sabha rejects a Bill introduced in Lok Sabha    | Bill fails at that stage; no inter-House deadlock |
| Lok Sabha passes a Bill, but Rajya Sabha rejects it | Deadlock under Article 108                        |
| Rajya Sabha passes a Bill, but Lok Sabha rejects it | Deadlock under Article 108                        |
| Both Houses pass the Bill in the same form          | No deadlock                                       |

Takeaway: Rejection by the House in which a Bill is introduced is different from rejection by the second House after the first House has passed it.

A deadlock arises when one House has passed the Bill but the other House rejects it, not merely whenever a Bill is rejected.

### 8.7 Lapsing of Bills in Parliament

A Bill is said to lapse when it ceases to be pending before Parliament and cannot proceed further in its existing form. If the government wishes to pursue it, the Bill must ordinarily be introduced afresh.

### 8.7.A Lapsing of Bills on dissolution of Lok Sabha

Under Article 107(4) and 107(5), the following rules apply:

| Status of Bill                                                                                   | Does it lapse on dissolution of Lok Sabha?              |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Bill pending in Lok Sabha (whether introduced there or transmitted from Rajya Sabha)             | Yes                                                     |
| Bill passed by Lok Sabha but pending in Rajya Sabha                                              | Yes                                                     |
| Bill pending in Rajya Sabha but not passed by Lok Sabha                                          | No                                                      |
| Bill passed by Rajya Sabha but pending in Lok Sabha                                              | Yes                                                     |
| Bill passed by both Houses and awaiting President's assent                                       | No                                                      |
| Bill returned by President for reconsideration and pending in Lok Sabha                          | Yes                                                     |
| Bill returned by President for reconsideration and pending only in Rajya Sabha                   | No, if it has not subsequently been passed by Lok Sabha |
| Bill for which the President has notified intention to summon a joint sitting before dissolution | No                                                      |

### 8.7.B Important distinctions

1\. Dissolution versus prorogation

- Dissolution of Lok Sabha causes certain pending Bills to lapse.
- Prorogation of either House does not cause pending Bills to lapse.
- Adjournment also does not cause Bills to lapse.

2\. Bills originating in Rajya Sabha

A Bill pending in Rajya Sabha that has not been passed by Lok Sabha does not lapse merely because Lok Sabha is dissolved.

However, if Rajya Sabha has passed the Bill and transmitted it to Lok Sabha, where it remains pending, it lapses.

3\. Bills passed by both Houses

A Bill passed by both Houses and awaiting the President's assent does not lapse on dissolution of Lok Sabha.

4\. Joint sitting

If the President has already notified the intention to summon a joint sitting under Article 108 before dissolution, the joint sitting may take place even after Lok Sabha is dissolved.

Merely satisfying the conditions for a joint sitting is not sufficient to prevent lapsing; the President's notification must have been issued.

### 8.7.C Special cases

- Money Bills: A Money Bill pending in Lok Sabha, or passed by Lok Sabha but pending in Rajya Sabha, lapses on dissolution of Lok Sabha.
- Constitutional Amendment Bills: Article 368 does not provide an exemption from the ordinary rules of lapsing. Such Bills are subject to the applicable rules under Article 107.
- Bills before parliamentary committees: Referral to a committee does not independently protect a Bill from lapsing. Its status in Parliament determines whether it lapses.

### 8.7.D Quick revision for UPSC Prelims

Remember these five rules:

1. Pending in Lok Sabha → Lapses.
2. Passed by Lok Sabha, pending in Rajya Sabha → Lapses.
3. Pending in Rajya Sabha, not passed by Lok Sabha → Does not lapse.
4. Passed by both Houses, awaiting President's assent → Does not lapse.
5. Joint sitting already notified by President → Does not lapse.

UPSC trap: A Bill passed by Rajya Sabha but pending in Lok Sabha lapses, whereas a Bill pending in Rajya Sabha that has not been passed by Lok Sabha does not lapse.

### 8.8 Types of Constitutional Amendments in India

The Indian Constitution provides for three broad methods of constitutional amendment, classified according to the majority and procedure required.

### 8.8.1 Amendment by Simple Majority of Parliament

Certain constitutional provisions can be changed by an ordinary law passed by a simple majority of members present and voting in Parliament.

Such changes are not treated as constitutional amendments under Article 368.

Important examples:

| Subject                                                                                                                 | Constitutional Article             |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Admission or establishment of new States                                                                                | Article 2                          |
| Formation of new States; alteration of State boundaries, areas or names                                                 | Article 3                          |
| Creation or abolition of State Legislative Councils                                                                     | Article 169                        |
| Citizenship provisions                                                                                                  | Article 11                         |
| Salaries and allowances of specified constitutional authorities, where Parliament is empowered to determine them by law | Relevant constitutional provisions |
| Parliamentary privileges, where Parliament legislates under the Constitution                                            | Article 105(3)                     |
| Use of English in Parliament after the prescribed constitutional period                                                 | Article 120                        |
| Quorum in Parliament, where altered by parliamentary law                                                                | Article 100(3)                     |
| Delimitation and readjustment of constituencies within the applicable constitutional framework                          | Articles 82 and 170                |

Important: Some of these laws may alter constitutional arrangements or provisions, but they are enacted outside the Article 368 amendment procedure.

### 8.8.2 Amendment by Special Majority of Parliament

Most provisions of the Constitution are amended under Article 368 by a special majority in each House separately.

The special majority requires both:

1. A majority of the total membership of the House.
2. At least two-thirds of members present and voting.

Important examples:

- Fundamental Rights.
- Directive Principles of State Policy.
- Fundamental Duties.
- Most provisions concerning the Union and State executive and legislature, except those requiring State ratification.
- Emergency provisions.
- Provisions concerning constitutional bodies where State ratification is not specifically required.

No joint sitting is permitted for a constitutional amendment Bill.

### 8.8.3 Amendment by Special Majority of Parliament and Ratification by States

Certain provisions affecting the federal structure require:

1. Special majority under Article 368 in each House of Parliament.
2. Ratification by the legislatures of at least half of the States, before the Bill is presented to the President for assent.

Important subjects requiring State ratification:

| Subject                                       | Constitutional provision |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Election of President                         | Articles 54 and 55       |
| Extent of executive power of Union and States | Articles 73 and 162      |
| Supreme Court                                 | Chapter IV of Part V     |
| High Courts                                   | Chapter V of Part VI     |
| High Courts for Union Territories             | Chapter I of Part VIII   |
| Distribution of legislative powers            | Chapter I of Part XI     |
| Lists in the Seventh Schedule                 | Seventh Schedule         |
| Representation of States in Parliament        | Article 368 proviso      |
| Amendment procedure itself                    | Article 368              |

Important distinctions:

- Ratification is required from at least half of the States, not Union Territories.
- State Legislatures ratify through resolutions; they do not amend the parliamentary Bill.
- The Constitution does not prescribe a special-majority requirement for State ratification. A simple majority is sufficient.
- There is no constitutional time limit for State ratification.

### 8.8.4 Special Cases: Articles 3 and 169

These two provisions are particularly important for UPSC Prelims because they involve States but do not require State ratification under Article 368.

| Feature                         | Article 3: State Reorganisation                                     | Article 169: State Legislative Council                           |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Purpose                         | Formation of States; alteration of State areas, boundaries or names | Creation or abolition of a State Legislative Council             |
| Initiation                      | Bill introduced in Parliament on President's recommendation         | Resolution initiated in the concerned State Legislative Assembly |
| State's role                    | State Legislature is given an opportunity to express its views      | State Assembly must first pass a special-majority resolution     |
| Is State's consent binding?     | No                                                                  | State Assembly resolution is a necessary prerequisite            |
| Parliamentary majority          | Simple majority                                                     | Simple majority                                                  |
| Article 368 procedure required? | No                                                                  | No                                                               |

Article 3: The President must refer the relevant proposal to the affected State Legislature for its views and specify a period for response. Parliament is not bound to accept those views.

Article 169: The State Legislative Assembly must pass a resolution supported by:

- A majority of the total membership of the Assembly; and
- At least two-thirds of members present and voting.

After this resolution, Parliament may create or abolish the Legislative Council through an ordinary law passed by a simple majority.

The State Assembly's resolution does not itself create or abolish the Council.

### 8.8.5 Comparison of the Three Amendment Methods

| Feature                 | Simple Majority                                                                 | Special Majority                | Special Majority + State Ratification |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Article 368 applicable? | No                                                                              | Yes                             | Yes                                   |
| Parliamentary majority  | Simple                                                                          | Special                         | Special                               |
| State ratification      | Not required                                                                    | Not required                    | At least half of States               |
| Example                 | Creation of Legislative Council under Article 169                               | Amendment of Fundamental Rights | Amendment of Seventh Schedule         |
| Joint sitting           | Not available for Article 368 Bills; ordinary legislative rules apply otherwise | Not permitted                   | Not permitted                         |

## 9. Budget and parliamentary financial control

### 9.1 Annual Financial Statement

Article 112 provides for the Annual Financial Statement, commonly called the Union Budget.

It presents estimated receipts and expenditure of the Government of India for the financial year.

### 9.2 Stages of budgetary approval

1. Presentation of the Budget.
2. General discussion.
3. Scrutiny of Demands for Grants by Department-related Standing Committees.
4. Voting on Demands for Grants in Lok Sabha.
5. Passing of the Appropriation Bill.
6. Passing of the Finance Bill.

### 9.3 Charged and voted expenditure

Charged expenditure on the Consolidated Fund of India is discussed but not voted upon.

Examples include the emoluments and allowances of the President and the salaries and allowances of Supreme Court judges.

Voted expenditure requires approval of Lok Sabha through Demands for Grants.

### 9.3.1 Expenditure Charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (CFI)

Article 112(3) of the Indian Constitution specifies the expenditures charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.

| 3   | Salaries and allowances of the Speaker and Deputy Speaker of Lok Sabha                                                                                                          | Article 112(3)(b)        |

### 9.3.2 Other important charged expenditures

The Constitution also provides for certain other charged expenditures, including:

| ------------------------ | ------------------------ | ---------------------- |

| Voting in Lok Sabha      | Not subject to vote      | Subject to vote        |

| Appropriation Act        | Required                 | Required               |

Under Articles 113 and 114, charged expenditure is not voted upon, but it still requires parliamentary appropriation before withdrawal from the CFI.


### 9.4.4 Important UPSC Prelims distinctions

- Supreme Court judges: Salaries, allowances and pensions are charged on the CFI.
- High Court judges: Salaries and allowances are charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State concerned, while pensions are charged on the CFI.
- UPSC: Its administrative expenses, including salaries, allowances and pensions of members and staff, are charged on the CFI.
- Election Commission of India: The salaries and administrative expenses of the Election Commission are not generally constitutionally charged on the CFI.
- MPs: Salaries and allowances of ordinary MPs are not charged expenditure merely because the salaries of the Speaker, Deputy Speaker, Chairman and Deputy Chairman are charged.

Most important distinction: Charged expenditure is discussable but not votable in Parliament. It is not exempt from the requirement of an Appropriation Act.


### 9.5 Cut Motions in the Indian Parliament

### 9.5.1 Meaning and purpose

A **Cut Motion** is a motion moved by a member of the **Lok Sabha** during the discussion on **Demands for Grants** to propose a reduction in the amount requested by the government.

Cut motions enable Lok Sabha members to:

- Express disapproval of a government policy.
- Suggest reductions in government expenditure.
- Highlight specific grievances relating to the responsibilities of the Union Government.

**Important:** Cut motions are moved in **Lok Sabha, not Rajya Sabha**, because Demands for Grants are voted upon only by Lok Sabha.

### 9.5.2 Types of Cut Motions

There are **three types of Cut Motions**:

| Cut Motion | Proposed reduction | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Policy Cut Motion** | Reduce the Demand to **₹1** | Express disapproval of the policy underlying the Demand |
| **Economy Cut Motion** | Reduce the Demand by a **specified amount** | Suggest economy in government expenditure |
| **Token Cut Motion** | Reduce the Demand by **₹100** | Highlight a specific grievance within the Union Government's responsibility |

### 9.5.3 Understanding Token Cut Motion

A **Token Cut Motion** seeks to reduce a particular Demand for Grant by **₹100** to highlight a specific grievance.

The reduction is **symbolic**. Its primary purpose is not to save ₹100 but to draw Parliament's attention to a grievance and seek the government's response.

**Example:**

Suppose the Ministry of Railways seeks a grant of ₹10,000 crore.

An MP believes that the government has failed to address a particular railway safety issue.

The MP may move a Token Cut Motion proposing a reduction of **₹100** in the Demand for Grant.

- The motion enables discussion of the specific grievance.
- If adopted, the Demand is reduced by ₹100.
- The adoption also represents a significant parliamentary defeat for the government.

**Why ₹100?**

The **Rules of Procedure of Lok Sabha** prescribe ₹100 as the reduction for a Token Cut Motion. The amount has no special economic significance.

### 9.6 What happens if a Cut Motion is passed?

The consequences must be distinguished between the **financial effect** and the **political effect**.

**A. Financial effect**

If a Cut Motion is adopted, the concerned Demand for Grant is reduced by the amount specified in the motion.

**B. Political effect**

- Passage of a Cut Motion represents a defeat of the government on a financial matter.
- It is conventionally regarded as an indication that the government has lost the confidence of Lok Sabha.
- Consequently, the **Council of Ministers is expected to resign** under the principles of parliamentary government and collective responsibility.

However, **Article 75(3) does not expressly provide that passage of a Cut Motion automatically terminates the Council of Ministers' tenure**.

The political consequence arises from parliamentary convention and the principle of collective responsibility.

### 9.7 Cut Motion vs No-Confidence Motion

| Feature | Cut Motion | No-Confidence Motion |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Challenge a policy, expenditure or highlight a grievance | Test whether the Council of Ministers enjoys Lok Sabha's confidence |
| **Subject** | A particular Demand for Grant | Council of Ministers as a whole |
| **When moved** | During consideration of Demands for Grants | Subject to Lok Sabha's prescribed procedure |
| **If passed** | Government suffers a financial defeat, conventionally treated as loss of confidence | Lok Sabha explicitly withdraws confidence from the government |
| **Resignation** | Expected under parliamentary convention | Council of Ministers must resign |
| **Constitutional principle** | Financial control and collective responsibility | Collective responsibility under Article 75(3) |

### 9.8 Important UPSC Prelims points

1. **Policy Cut Motion:** Reduction of a Demand to **₹1**, indicating disapproval of the underlying policy.
2. **Economy Cut Motion:** Reduction by a **specified amount**, suggesting economy in expenditure.
3. **Token Cut Motion:** Reduction by **₹100**, highlighting a specific grievance.
4. Cut Motions are moved only in **Lok Sabha**, not Rajya Sabha.
5. Cut Motions apply to **votable Demands for Grants**, not expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.
6. **Moving a Cut Motion does not mean it has been passed.** The House must adopt it.
7. Passage of a Cut Motion is conventionally regarded as a **loss of confidence in the government**, although the Constitution does not expressly prescribe automatic resignation upon its adoption.

### 9.9 Other financial mechanisms

- Vote on Account: Advance grant for expenditure pending completion of the regular budget procedure.
- Vote of Credit: Grant for an unexpected demand whose details cannot be stated in the ordinary budget form.
- Exceptional Grant: Grant for a special purpose outside the current service of a financial year.
- Supplementary Grant: Additional amount when the original allocation is insufficient.
- Excess Grant: Parliamentary regularisation of expenditure exceeding the grant for a completed financial year.


### 9.10 Interim Budget vs Vote on Account

An Interim Budget and a Vote on Account are related but distinct concepts. Both are particularly relevant when a general election is approaching and the outgoing government does not present a regular full-year Budget.

### 9.10.1 Key differences

| Feature                | Interim Budget                                                                                       | Vote on Account                                                                                                      |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Meaning                | A Budget presented for a transitional period, usually before general elections                       | Advance parliamentary authorisation for government expenditure pending completion of the regular budgetary procedure |
| Constitutional basis   | No separate constitutional provision specifically defines an Interim Budget                          | Article 116(1)(a)                                                                                                    |
| Scope                  | Contains estimates of government receipts and expenditure, including revenue and fiscal projections  | Concerns expenditure only                                                                                            |
| Purpose                | Presents the government's financial position and Budget estimates during a transition                | Enables the government to meet expenditure before the regular Appropriation Act is passed                            |

| Parliamentary approval | Relevant Budget proposals require applicable parliamentary approval                                  | Lok Sabha votes an advance grant; an Appropriation Act authorises withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund of India     |
| Nature                 | A type of Budget presentation                                                                        | A specific constitutional financial mechanism                                                                        |

### 9.10.2 Relationship between Interim Budget and Vote on Account

An Interim Budget may include a request for a Vote on Account.

Example: Interim Budget 2024–25

- The Union Government presented the Interim Budget on 1 February 2024, before the Lok Sabha general elections.
- Parliament approved a Vote on Account to meet expenditure during the initial months of the financial year.
- The new government subsequently presented the full Union Budget on 23 July 2024.

Thus, the Interim Budget provided the broader financial statement, while the Vote on Account enabled expenditure during the intervening period.

### 9.10.3 Important UPSC Prelims points

- An Interim Budget is not separately defined in the Constitution.
- A Vote on Account is expressly provided under Article 116.
- A Vote on Account can be used even when no Interim Budget is presented.
- An Interim Budget contains both receipts and expenditure estimates; a Vote on Account authorises expenditure.
- A Vote on Account does not itself authorise taxation or replace the regular Budget.
- There is no constitutional requirement that a Vote on Account must cover exactly two months.
- A Vote on Account is not the same as a Vote of Credit, which is intended for unexpected expenditure demands of an indefinite character or magnitude.

### 9.11 Funds of the Government of India

The Constitution provides for three important funds/accounts for managing government finances:

1. Consolidated Fund of India
2. Public Account of India
3. Contingency Fund of India

### 9.11.1 Consolidated Fund of India — Article 266(1)

The Consolidated Fund of India is the principal fund of the Central Government.

Receipts credited to the fund:

- All revenues received by the Government of India, including tax and non-tax revenues.
- All loans raised by the Government of India.
- All money received in repayment of loans given by the Government of India.

Expenditure:

- Government expenditure, including salaries, pensions, defence and development programmes.
- Both voted and charged expenditure.

Parliamentary control:

- Money cannot be withdrawn except through appropriation made in accordance with law and the Constitution.
- Voted expenditure requires approval of Lok Sabha through Demands for Grants.
- Charged expenditure is not voted upon but is included in the Appropriation Act.

### 9.11.2 Public Account of India — Article 266(2)

The Public Account records other public money received by or on behalf of the Government of India, which does not form part of the Consolidated Fund.

Examples include:

- Provident fund deposits.
- Small savings collections.
- Judicial deposits.
- Other deposits and remittances.

Parliamentary control:

- Parliamentary appropriation is not required for withdrawals from the Public Account.
- Withdrawals are governed by applicable laws and rules.
- The Public Account remains subject to government accounting, audit and parliamentary oversight.

UPSC trap: Money in the Public Account is not necessarily revenue belonging to the government. Much of it represents money held on behalf of others.

### 9.11.3 Contingency Fund of India — Article 267(1)

The Contingency Fund of India is an imprest fund established by parliamentary law to meet unforeseen expenditure requiring immediate attention.

- It is placed at the disposal of the President.
- Advances may be made pending parliamentary authorisation.
- The expenditure is subsequently authorised by Parliament.
- The fund is replenished through the prescribed appropriation process.

Current corpus: ₹30,000 crore, following the increase provided for in 2021.

UPSC trap: The Contingency Fund enables urgent advances before parliamentary approval. It does not permanently exempt the expenditure from parliamentary authorisation.

### 9.11.4 Comparison of the three funds

| Feature                                    | Consolidated Fund of India                              | Public Account of India                        | Contingency Fund of India                        |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Constitutional Article                     | 266(1)                                                  | 266(2)                                         | 267(1)                                           |
| Nature                                     | Principal government fund                               | Other public money held by government          | Imprest for unforeseen expenditure               |
| Main receipts                              | Revenue, borrowings, loan repayments                    | Deposits, provident funds, small savings, etc. | Corpus established through parliamentary law     |
| Parliamentary appropriation for withdrawal | Required                                                | Not required                                   | Advance first; subsequent authorisation required |
| Purpose                                    | Regular government receipts and expenditure             | Custody and accounting of other public money   | Immediate unforeseen expenditure                 |
| Constitutional authority                   | Government expenditure subject to parliamentary control | Government holds and accounts for the money    | Placed at disposal of President                  |

### 9.11.5 Corresponding funds at the State level

| Union                      | State                          | Article |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------- |
| Consolidated Fund of India | Consolidated Fund of the State | 266(1)  |
| Public Account of India    | Public Account of the State    | 266(2)  |
| Contingency Fund of India  | Contingency Fund of the State  | 267     |

The State Contingency Fund is placed at the disposal of the Governor, whereas the Union Contingency Fund is placed at the disposal of the President.

### 9.11.6 Important UPSC Prelims distinctions

- Consolidated Fund: Parliamentary appropriation is necessary for withdrawals.
- Public Account: Parliamentary appropriation is not required for withdrawals.
- Contingency Fund: Immediate advances are permitted, followed by parliamentary authorisation.
- Charged expenditure: Not voted upon, but withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund still requires appropriation.
- Borrowings: Loans raised by the Government of India are credited to the Consolidated Fund, not the Public Account.
- Provident fund deposits: Generally accounted for in the Public Account, not the Consolidated Fund.

## 10. Parliamentary devices and government accountability

### 10.1 Question Hour

Question Hour is the period during which MPs question ministers about government policies, decisions and administration.

It is governed by the rules of the respective Houses, not directly by a specific constitutional article.

| Type                       | Answer                      | Supplementary questions         |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Starred question           | Oral                        | Allowed                         |
| Unstarred question         | Written                     | Not allowed                     |
| Short-notice question      | Oral                        | Allowed                         |
| Question to private member | As provided under the rules | Subject to applicable procedure |

A short-notice question concerns a matter of urgent public importance and is asked with notice shorter than the ordinary notice period.

Current practice: In Lok Sabha, Question Hour normally occupies the first hour of a sitting (11 a.m. to 12 noon). In Rajya Sabha, it ordinarily takes place from 12 noon to 1 PM.

### 10.2 Zero Hour

Zero Hour is a parliamentary practice during which members raise urgent matters of public importance.

- It is not expressly mentioned in the Constitution.
- It is not the same as Question Hour.
- It follows Question Hour in Lok Sabha under ordinary scheduling.
- It is regulated by the presiding officer and the applicable parliamentary procedures.

### 10.3 Important motions and devices

| Device                    | Purpose                                                                | Important distinction                   |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| No-confidence motion      | Tests confidence of Lok Sabha in Council of Ministers                  | Lok Sabha only                          |
| Confidence motion         | Government seeks affirmation of majority support                       | Lok Sabha                               |
| Censure motion            | Expresses disapproval of government policy or conduct                  | May specify grounds                     |
| Adjournment motion        | Interrupts normal business to discuss a definite, urgent public matter | Lok Sabha procedure; element of censure |
| Calling Attention         | Seeks ministerial statement on urgent public matter                    | Available under rules of both Houses    |
| Short-duration discussion | Discussion of urgent public importance                                 | No formal motion or vote                |
| Privilege motion          | Raises alleged breach of parliamentary privilege                       | Subject to presiding officer's decision |
| Point of order            | Draws attention to an alleged breach of rules or procedure             | Not a general debate                    |

### 10.4 No-confidence motion

Under Article 75(3), the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to Lok Sabha.

- A no-confidence motion need not state specific reasons.
- It requires support of at least 50 members for leave to be granted under Lok Sabha rules.
- It is decided by a simple majority of members present and voting.
- If carried, the entire Council of Ministers must resign.

A motion against an individual minister is not the same as a no-confidence motion against the Council of Ministers.

### 10.5 Adjournment motion and Calling Attention

An adjournment motion concerns a specific, recent and urgent matter of public importance involving the responsibility of the Union government.

It requires the Speaker's consent and the prescribed support. If admitted, it interrupts the normal business of Lok Sabha.

Calling Attention is a distinct device for obtaining an authoritative statement from a minister. It does not involve a formal motion or vote.

Both are established parliamentary procedures, although their actual use varies from session to session.

- There are 24 Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committees.
- Each ordinarily consists of 31 members: 21 from Lok Sabha and 10 from Rajya Sabha.
- They examine Demands for Grants, referred Bills, annual reports and long-term policy documents.
- Ministers cannot be members.
- Their recommendations are generally advisory, not binding.

Of the 24 committees, 16 function under the administrative control of the Lok Sabha Speaker and eight under the Rajya Sabha Chairman.

### 11.3 Select Committee and Joint Parliamentary Committee

A Select Committee on a Bill consists of members of one House.

A Joint Committee on a Bill consists of members from both Houses.

They examine Bills referred to them and submit reports with recommendations. Parliament is not bound to accept every recommendation.

A Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC) may also be constituted for a specific investigation or other assigned purpose.

UPSC distinction: A Joint Committee examining a Bill is not the same as a joint sitting of Parliament under Article 108.

## 12. Parliamentary privileges

Under Article 105, important parliamentary privileges include:

- Freedom of speech in Parliament, subject to constitutional provisions and parliamentary rules.
- Immunity in respect of anything said or any vote given in Parliament or its committees.
- Protection concerning publication of parliamentary proceedings under the conditions specified in Article 105.
- Other privileges recognised under the constitutional framework.

These privileges exist to enable Parliament to discharge its functions independently.

They are not unlimited personal exemptions from ordinary law.

### Important judicial development

In Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024), the Supreme Court held that legislators cannot claim parliamentary privilege as immunity from prosecution for bribery connected with a speech or vote in the legislature.

This decision overruled the relevant majority position in P. V. Narasimha Rao v. State (CBI/SPE) (1998).

## 13. Delimitation and parliamentary representation

### 13.1 Meaning of delimitation

Delimitation refers to fixing or readjusting the boundaries of territorial constituencies for elections.

Two separate matters must be understood:

1. Allocation of seats among States: Determines how many Lok Sabha seats each State receives.
2. Delimitation within States: Determines the territorial boundaries of individual constituencies.

Article 82 provides for readjustment after each census through an authority and procedure determined by parliamentary law.

### 13.2 Delimitation Commission

The Delimitation Commission is a statutory body constituted under a Delimitation Act.

Its functions, as authorised by the relevant law, include:

- Determining or readjusting constituency boundaries.
- Ensuring constituencies have broadly comparable populations within the applicable legal framework.
- Identifying constituencies reserved for SCs and STs according to applicable provisions.
- Publishing delimitation orders.

The Commission does not possess an independent, unlimited constitutional power to increase the number of Lok Sabha seats.

Seat allocation and constituency delimitation are distinct functions, although a delimitation law may provide for both.

### 13.3 Constitutional freeze

The 42nd Constitutional Amendment froze readjustment of Lok Sabha seats among States on the basis of the 1971 Census.

The 84th Amendment extended the freeze until the relevant figures from the first census taken after 2026 become available.

The 87th Amendment permitted constituency boundaries to be readjusted using the 2001 Census without changing the frozen allocation of seats among States.

Therefore, the present framework distinguishes:

- Seat allocation among States: based on the 1971 Census under the freeze.
- Territorial constituency boundaries: last generally readjusted using the 2001 Census.

## 14. Women's reservation in legislatures

### 14.1 Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023

The Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam provides for reservation of approximately one-third of seats for women in:

- Lok Sabha.
- State Legislative Assemblies.
- Legislative Assembly of the National Capital Territory of Delhi.

The reservation also applies within seats reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

### 14.2 Implementation and duration

The constitutional framework links the commencement of reservation to delimitation following the relevant census figures after the amendment.

The amendment provides for a 15-year period from commencement, subject to continuation by parliamentary law.

The existence of the constitutional amendment should not be confused with the actual operation of reserved seats in an election.

### 14.3 Rajya Sabha distinction

The 106th Amendment does not provide one-third reservation for women in Rajya Sabha or State Legislative Councils.

## 18. Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 2023

The Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 2023 enables the Central Government to give immediate, temporary legal effect to certain tax proposals contained in Bills introduced in Parliament, even before those Bills are enacted.

- Act: No. 50 of 2023
- Enacted: 28 December 2023
- Replaced: Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 1931
- Applicable taxes: Customs duties and excise duties.

### 18.1 Purpose

Ordinarily, a tax proposal contained in a Bill does not automatically become law merely because the Bill has been introduced.

However, delaying the implementation of proposed increases in customs or excise duties may encourage advance imports, stockpiling or other attempts to avoid higher duties.

The Act allows specified tax proposals to take effect temporarily while Parliament considers the Bill.

### 18.22 Important provisions

| Feature                      | Provision                                                                                                                  |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Applicable proposals         | Imposition or increase of customs or excise duties                                                                         |
| Declaration                  | Central Government may include a declaration in the Bill stating that immediate effect is expedient in the public interest |
| Effective date               | Immediately after the day on which the Bill is introduced expires                                                          |
| Maximum provisional duration | 75 days after the day of introduction                                                                                      |
| Parliamentary approval       | Bill still requires enactment through the normal legislative process                                                       |
| Refund                       | Provided in specified circumstances when the provisional provision is amended or ceases to operate                         |

A declared provision ceases to have provisional effect when it becomes law, when the Central Government issues the prescribed notification pursuant to a parliamentary motion, or upon expiry of the 75-day period, whichever occurs first.

### 18.3 Example

Suppose a Finance Bill introduced on 1 February proposes increasing customs duty on a particular product from 10% to 15%.

If the Bill contains the required declaration under the Act:

- The proposed 15% duty can take effect from 2 February.
- The government need not wait for the entire Finance Bill to become law.
- The provisional effect is limited to the period prescribed by the Act.
- If Parliament subsequently enacts a lower duty, applicable excess collections are subject to the statutory refund provisions.

### 18.4 Important UPSC Prelims points

- The Act applies specifically to the imposition or increase of customs and excise duties, not automatically to all taxes.
- The Central Government's declaration is necessary; merely introducing a Finance Bill does not activate the Act.
- The declared provision takes effect from the day following introduction, not from the date of presidential assent to the Bill.
- The provisional operation cannot continue beyond the prescribed 75-day limit under this Act.
- The Act does not eliminate Parliament's legislative authority.
- The 2023 Act repealed and replaced the 1931 Act while substantially retaining its mechanism.

### Q39. Legislative bribery
