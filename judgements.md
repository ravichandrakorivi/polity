# Important Supreme Court Judgments in India

These notes cover landmark Supreme Court judgments important for UPSC CSE Prelims, particularly Fundamental Rights, the Basic Structure Doctrine, Parliament, the judiciary, federalism, reservations, elections, local governance and constitutional institutions.

For each case, focus on the year, constitutional article involved, principle established and subsequent developments. Several earlier judgments have been modified or overruled, making the distinction between historical and current legal positions especially important.

## 1. Basic Structure Doctrine and constitutional amendments

### 1.1 Shankari Prasad v. Union of India (1951)

Issue: Whether Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights.

Judgment:

- Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights through Article 368.
- A constitutional amendment was not considered ordinary law within the meaning of Article 13(2).

Prelims point: Initially, the Supreme Court upheld Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights.

### 1.2 Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965)

Issue: Validity of the 17th Constitutional Amendment.

Judgment:

- Reaffirmed Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights.
- Followed the reasoning of Shankari Prasad.

### 1.3 I. C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)

Issue: Whether Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights.

Judgment:

- Held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights so as to take them away or abridge them.
- Applied the doctrine of prospective overruling.

Prelims point: This position was subsequently changed by the Kesavananda Bharati judgment.

### 1.4 Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

Issue: Extent of Parliament's constitutional amendment power.

Judgment:

- Established the Basic Structure Doctrine.
- Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights.
- However, Parliament cannot destroy or alter the Constitution's basic structure.

Examples of features recognised as part of the basic structure include:

- Supremacy of the Constitution.
- Rule of law.
- Judicial review.
- Independence of the judiciary.
- Federalism.
- Secularism.
- Democracy and republican government.
- Separation of powers.

Prelims points:

- Decided by a 13-judge Bench, the largest in Supreme Court history.
- Judgment delivered on 24 April 1973.
- The Constitution does not contain an exhaustive definition of basic structure.

### 1.5 Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)

Issue: Constitutional validity of provisions seeking to protect certain high-level elections from judicial scrutiny.

Judgment:

- Invalidated the relevant provision introduced by the 39th Constitutional Amendment.
- Recognised the importance of free and fair elections and judicial review in the constitutional framework.

### 1.6 Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

Issue: Validity of provisions of the 42nd Constitutional Amendment.

Judgment:

- Struck down provisions attempting to make Parliament's amending power unlimited.
- Reaffirmed that limited amending power is part of the basic structure.
- Emphasised harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

Prelims point: Parliament cannot use Article 368 to destroy the limitations on its own amendment power.

### 1.7 Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)

Issue: Judicial review of laws placed in the Ninth Schedule.

Judgment:

- Treated 24 April 1973 as the relevant dividing date.
- Ninth Schedule insertions after that date are subject to basic-structure scrutiny.

### 1.8 I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

Issue: Whether laws placed in the Ninth Schedule are completely immune from judicial review.

Judgment:

- Laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 may be reviewed for violations affecting the basic structure.
- Ninth Schedule inclusion does not provide absolute constitutional immunity.

## 2. Fundamental Rights and personal liberty

### 2.1 A. K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950)

Issue: Interpretation of personal liberty under Article 21.

Judgment:

- Adopted a relatively narrow interpretation of Article 21.
- Treated Fundamental Rights largely as separate constitutional guarantees.
- Accepted the importance of procedure established by law.

Current position: Its restrictive approach was substantially displaced by Maneka Gandhi (1978).

### 2.2 Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)

Issue: Whether the government could impound a passport without adequate procedural safeguards.

Judgment:

- Expanded the interpretation of Article 21.
- Procedure affecting life or personal liberty must be fair, just and reasonable.
- Established the close relationship between Articles 14, 19 and 21.

Prelims point: Article 21 cannot be interpreted independently of the constitutional guarantees of equality and freedom.

### 2.3 Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979)

Issue: Prolonged detention of undertrial prisoners.

Judgment:

- Recognised the right to a speedy trial as part of Article 21.
- Highlighted the constitutional importance of legal aid and access to justice.

### 2.4 Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi (1981)

Issue: Meaning of the right to life.

Judgment:

- Held that Article 21 includes the right to live with human dignity.
- Recognised the importance of basic necessities and humane conditions.

### 2.5 Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985)

Issue: Eviction of pavement dwellers.

Judgment:

- Recognised the right to livelihood as an aspect of the right to life under Article 21.
- Did not establish an unconditional right to occupy public pavements.

### 2.6 D. K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997)

Issue: Custodial violence and arbitrary arrest.

Judgment:

- Laid down safeguards against custodial torture and abuse.
- Emphasised arrest and detention procedures consistent with Articles 21 and 22.

### 2.7 Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017)

Issue: Whether privacy is a Fundamental Right.

Judgment:

- A nine-judge Bench unanimously recognised the right to privacy as a Fundamental Right.
- Privacy is protected principally under Article 21 and also through other Fundamental Rights.
- Overruled earlier decisions to the extent that they denied constitutional protection to privacy.

Prelims point: Privacy is a Fundamental Right, but it is not absolute.

### 2.8 Common Cause v. Union of India (2018)

Issue: Right to die with dignity and withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment.

Judgment:

- Recognised the right to die with dignity in the context of terminal illness and end-of-life decisions.
- Permitted advance medical directives or living wills subject to safeguards.

Prelims distinction: The judgment did not create an unrestricted right to assisted suicide.

### 2.9 Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020)

Issue: Restrictions on internet access in Jammu and Kashmir.

Judgment:

- Recognised that freedom of speech and expression and the freedom to practise a profession or trade through the internet enjoy constitutional protection.
- Held that internet restrictions must satisfy constitutional standards, including proportionality.
- Indefinite internet shutdowns are impermissible.

Prelims distinction: The Court did not declare internet access itself an independent Fundamental Right in all circumstances.

## 3. Equality, reservations and social justice

### 3.1 State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951)

Issue: Conflict between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

Judgment:

- Held that Directive Principles could not override enforceable Fundamental Rights.
- Led to the First Constitutional Amendment, which inserted Article 15(4).

Prelims point: Article 15(4) enables special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes and SCs/STs.

### 3.2 Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992)

Popular name: Mandal Commission case.

Judgment:

- Upheld 27% reservation for Other Backward Classes in Central Government services.
- Recognised exclusion of the creamy layer among OBCs.
- Established the general principle that reservations should ordinarily not exceed 50%, subject to exceptional circumstances.
- Held that Article 16(4), as then interpreted, did not permit reservation in promotion.

Subsequent development: The 77th Constitutional Amendment inserted Article 16(4A), enabling specified SC/ST promotion reservations.

### 3.3 M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006)

Issue: Constitutional validity of amendments concerning reservation in promotion.

Judgment:

- Upheld the relevant constitutional amendments.
- Required constitutional safeguards when States provide reservation in promotion.
- Emphasised administrative efficiency under Article 335.

Subsequent development: The requirement to collect quantifiable data proving SC/ST backwardness was removed in Jarnail Singh (2018).

### 3.4 Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta (2018)

Judgment:

- Clarified the application of M. Nagaraj.
- Held that States need not collect quantifiable data to establish the backwardness of SCs/STs for promotion reservations.
- Retained other applicable constitutional requirements, including assessment of inadequate representation.

### 3.5 Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022)

Issue: Constitutional validity of the 103rd Amendment introducing EWS reservation.

Judgment:

- Upheld the 103rd Constitutional Amendment by a 3:2 majority.
- Sustained the constitutional provisions enabling up to 10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections.

Prelims point: EWS reservation is based on Articles 15(6) and 16(6).

### 3.6 State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024)

Issue: Whether Scheduled Castes may be sub-classified for reservation benefits.

Judgment:

- A seven-judge Bench, by a 6:1 majority, permitted constitutionally justified sub-classification within Scheduled Castes for reservation.
- Overruled the contrary position in E. V. Chinnaiah (2004).
- Such sub-classification must satisfy constitutional standards and be supported by appropriate evidence.

Prelims distinction: Sub-classification for distributing reservation benefits is not the same as a State independently altering the Presidential list of Scheduled Castes under Article 341.

## 4. Parliament, elections and democratic institutions

### 4.1 Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992)

Issue: Validity of the Anti-Defection Law under the Tenth Schedule.

Judgment:

- Upheld the broad constitutional validity of the Anti-Defection Law.
- Recognised judicial review of decisions made by the Speaker or Chairman concerning disqualification.

Prelims point: The Speaker's decision under the Tenth Schedule is not completely immune from judicial review.

### 4.2 Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms (2002)

Issue: Voters' access to information about election candidates.

Judgment:

- Recognised voters' right to know relevant information about candidates.
- Supported disclosure of candidates' criminal antecedents, assets, liabilities and educational qualifications.

### 4.3 PUCL v. Union of India (2013)

Issue: Whether voters should have an option to reject all candidates without selecting any.

Judgment:

\- Directed introduction of NOTA — None of the Above in electronic voting machines.

Prelims trap: NOTA does not automatically invalidate an election when it receives more votes than any individual candidate.

### 4.4 Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013)

Issue: Disqualification of convicted legislators.

Judgment:

- Struck down Section 8(4) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951.
- Removed the special statutory protection that had allowed sitting legislators to retain their seats temporarily despite disqualifying convictions.

Prelims point: Disqualification is governed by the applicable statutory provisions; not every criminal conviction necessarily results in disqualification.

### 4.5 Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024)

Issue: Whether legislators enjoy constitutional immunity from prosecution for accepting bribes in connection with legislative votes or speeches.

Judgment:

- A seven-judge Bench held that parliamentary privileges under Articles 105 and 194 do not protect legislators from prosecution for bribery.
- Overruled the relevant immunity recognised in P. V. Narasimha Rao v. State (1998).

Prelims point: Legislative freedom of speech and voting privileges do not confer immunity for bribery.&#x20;

### 4.6 Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2024) — Electoral Bonds

Issue: Constitutionality of the Electoral Bonds Scheme.

Judgment:

- Struck down the Electoral Bonds Scheme.
- Held that anonymous political funding through the scheme violated voters' right to information under Article 19(1)(a).
- Invalidated associated legal provisions facilitating the unconstitutional framework.

Prelims point: The judgment connects political funding transparency with the constitutional right to information.&#x20;

## 5. Federalism, Governors and Centre–State relations

### 5.1 State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963)

Issue: Extent of Union legislative power concerning State-owned property.

Judgment:

- Upheld Parliament's competence in the matter under consideration.
- Reaffirmed that Indian States do not possess sovereignty independent of the Constitution.

### 5.2 Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)

Issue: Nature of executive powers of the President and Governor.

Judgment:

- The President and Governor are constitutional heads.
- They ordinarily exercise executive functions on the aid and advice of their respective Councils of Ministers, except where the Constitution provides otherwise.

### 5.3 S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

Issue: Misuse of President's Rule under Article 356.

Judgment:

- Presidential proclamations under Article 356 are subject to judicial review.
- Majority support should ordinarily be tested on the floor of the Legislative Assembly.
- Recognised secularism as a basic feature of the Constitution.
- Established important limitations on arbitrary use of President's Rule.

Prelims point: A Governor's subjective assessment cannot ordinarily substitute for an available Assembly floor test.

### 5.4 B. P. Singhal v. Union of India (2010)

Issue: Removal of Governors before completion of their normal five-year term.

Judgment:

- Governors hold office during the President's pleasure.
- However, removal cannot be arbitrary, capricious or unreasonable.
- A change in the Union Government does not by itself justify arbitrary removal.

### 5.5 Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016)

Issue: Governor's role in the functioning of a State Legislature.

Judgment:

- Emphasised constitutional limitations on the Governor's discretionary authority.
- Held that the Governor cannot generally interfere in legislative proceedings outside the constitutional framework.

Prelims point: The Governor does not have an unrestricted power to direct the business of the Legislative Assembly.

### 5.6 Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018 and 2023)

Issue: Distribution of powers between the elected Delhi Government and the Lieutenant Governor.

Judgments:

- Emphasised representative government and constitutional accountability.
- Clarified that the Lieutenant Governor does not possess a general veto over every decision of the elected government.
- The 2023 Constitution Bench interpreted the elected Delhi Government's authority over services within the constitutional framework then before it.

Important subsequent development: Parliamentary legislation in 2023 altered the statutory framework governing services in Delhi. Therefore, the 2023 judgment should not be read without considering subsequent legislation and litigation.

### 5.7 In re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India (2025)

Issue: Constitutional powers under Articles 200 and 201 concerning State Bills.

This was an advisory opinion under Article 143, following the earlier 2025 Tamil Nadu Governor litigation.

Important conclusions:

- Courts cannot impose universally binding timelines on Governors or the President for deciding assent to Bills where the Constitution prescribes none.
- Courts cannot use Article 142 to grant deemed assent in place of the constitutionally designated authority.
- Prolonged and unexplained gubernatorial inaction may attract limited judicial intervention to require a decision, without substituting the Court's decision on assent.
- Where the Governor withholds assent to a Bill, the constitutional requirement of returning the Bill for reconsideration must be observed.

Prelims point: The 2025 Presidential Reference is particularly important for distinguishing constitutional obligations, judicial review and the limits of judicially prescribed deadlines.&#x20;

## 6. Judiciary, judicial appointments and judicial independence

### 6.1 S. P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981)

Popular name: First Judges Case.

Judgment:

- Gave primacy to the executive in the judicial appointments process as interpreted at that time.
- Its position on appointments was subsequently overruled.

### 6.2 Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (1993)

Popular name: Second Judges Case.

Judgment:

- Established the collegium system for judicial appointments.
- Gave primacy to the opinion of the Chief Justice of India, formed through consultation with senior judges.

### 6.3 In re Special Reference No. 1 of 1998

Popular name: Third Judges Case.

Judgment:

- Clarified the consultation process under the collegium system.
- Established the requirement of consultation with the four senior-most Supreme Court judges for appointments to the Supreme Court.

### 6.4 Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015)

Popular name: NJAC Case or Fourth Judges Case.

Judgment:

- Declared the 99th Constitutional Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act unconstitutional.
- Held that the proposed arrangement violated the basic structure, particularly judicial independence.

Prelims point: The collegium system continues; the NJAC is not operational.

## 7. Gender justice, dignity and individual freedoms

### 7.1 Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997)

Issue: Sexual harassment of women at workplaces.

Judgment:

- Established the Vishaka Guidelines to prevent and address workplace sexual harassment.
- Relied on constitutional guarantees and international conventions.

Subsequent development: Parliament enacted the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013.

### 7.2 National Legal Services Authority v. Union of India (2014)

Popular name: NALSA Judgment.

Judgment:

- Recognised transgender persons' constitutional rights.
- Recognised the right to self-identify one's gender.
- Affirmed protection under Articles 14, 15, 16, 19 and 21.

### 7.3 Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017)

Issue: Validity of instant triple talaq (talaq-e-biddat).

Judgment:

\- By a 3:2 majority, invalidated the practice of instant triple talaq.

Subsequent development: Parliament enacted the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019.

### 7.4 Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018)

Issue: Criminalisation of consensual same-sex relations under Section 377 of the IPC.

Judgment:

- Decriminalised consensual same-sex sexual conduct between adults.
- Affirmed constitutional protections of dignity, privacy, equality and autonomy.

Prelims distinction: Decriminalisation of consensual same-sex relations is not the same as legal recognition of same-sex marriage.

### 7.5 Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018)

Issue: Constitutionality of the adultery offence under Section 497 of the IPC.

Judgment:

- Struck down the criminal offence of adultery.
- Emphasised equality, dignity and individual autonomy.

Prelims point: Adultery is no longer a criminal offence, but may remain relevant in matrimonial proceedings.

### 7.6 Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2018)

Popular name: Sabarimala Case.

Judgment:

\- By a 4:1 majority, held the exclusion of women of the relevant age group from the Sabarimala temple unconstitutional.

Important: Subsequent review and larger constitutional questions concerning religious freedom and equality have been the subject of further proceedings. The original judgment should not be treated as the final resolution of every related legal question.

### 7.7 Supriyo v. Union of India (2023)

Issue: Legal recognition of same-sex marriage.

Judgment:

- The Supreme Court did not recognise a general Fundamental Right to marry a person of the same sex.
- Declined to judicially rewrite the Special Marriage Act to provide same-sex marriage recognition.
- Left major questions of legal recognition and associated benefits to the appropriate constitutional institutions.

Prelims distinction: The Navtej Singh Johar judgment protects consensual same-sex relationships from criminalisation; Supriyo did not legalise same-sex marriage.

## 8. Environment and sustainable development

### 8.1 M. C. Mehta v. Union of India (1986–1987) — Oleum Gas Leak

Judgment:

- Developed the principle of absolute liability for enterprises engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activities.
- Established a stricter standard than the traditional rule of strict liability.

### 8.2 Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991)

Judgment:

\- Recognised that the right to life under Article 21 includes enjoyment of pollution-free water and air.

### 8.3 Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996)

Judgment:

- Recognised the Precautionary Principle and Polluter Pays Principle as important components of Indian environmental law.
- Linked environmental protection with sustainable development.

### 8.4 M. C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath (1996)

Judgment:

- Applied the Public Trust Doctrine.
- Recognised that the State holds certain natural resources in trust for public use and benefit.

### 8.5 T. N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India (1996 onwards)

Significance:

- Major continuing litigation concerning forest conservation and governance.
- Expanded the practical interpretation of forests for regulatory purposes, subject to subsequent legal developments.

### 8.6 M. K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India (2024)

Issue: Protection of the Great Indian Bustard and regulation of power infrastructure in its habitat.

Judgment:

- Recognised a constitutional right against the adverse effects of climate change under Articles 14 and 21.
- Emphasised the need to balance biodiversity conservation and climate-related energy objectives.

## 9. Important judgments on contemporary constitutional issues

### 9.1 In re Article 370 of the Constitution (2023)

Issue: Constitutional validity of the 2019 changes to the special status of Jammu and Kashmir.

Judgment:

- Upheld the constitutional validity of the abrogation of Article 370.
- Recognised Article 370 as a temporary constitutional provision.
- Directed steps towards restoring electoral democracy, including Assembly elections.

Prelims point: The Supreme Court upheld the 2019 Article 370 changes; this does not mean Article 370 was originally absent from the Constitution.

### 9.2 Union of India v. Mohit Minerals (2022)

Issue: GST and the authority of GST Council recommendations.

Judgment:

- Held that recommendations of the GST Council are not legally binding on Parliament and State Legislatures.
- Emphasised the federal character of GST decision-making.

Relevant Article: 279A.

### 9.3 Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021)

Issue: Validity of the 97th Constitutional Amendment concerning co-operative societies.

Judgment:

- Partially invalidated provisions relating to co-operative societies operating exclusively within States because the necessary State ratification had not been obtained.
- The relevant framework continues for multi-State co-operative societies.

Prelims point: The entire 97th Amendment was not struck down.

### 9.4 Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra (2024)

Issue: Meaning of material resources of the community under Article 39(b).

Judgment:

- Held that not all privately owned property automatically constitutes a material resource of the community.
- Whether a resource falls within Article 39(b) depends on its characteristics and the relevant constitutional considerations.
- Clarified the continuing operation of the original protection under Article 31C concerning laws implementing Articles 39(b) and 39(c).

Prelims point: Article 39(b) does not automatically bring every private asset within the expression material resources of the community.

## 11. High-priority UPSC Prelims traps

1. Golaknath versus Kesavananda Bharati: Golaknath restricted amendment of Fundamental Rights; Kesavananda permitted amendments subject to the Basic Structure Doctrine.
2. Maneka Gandhi versus Puttaswamy: The former expanded fair procedure under Article 21; the latter expressly recognised privacy as a Fundamental Right.
3. Indra Sawhney versus Janhit Abhiyan: The former concerned OBC reservations; the latter upheld the EWS constitutional amendment.
4. M. Nagaraj versus Jarnail Singh: Jarnail Singh modified the earlier requirement relating to proof of SC/ST backwardness in promotion reservations.
5. Second Judges Case versus NJAC Case: The former established the collegium system; the latter invalidated the proposed NJAC framework.
6. S. R. Bommai versus B. P. Singhal: The former concerns President's Rule; the latter concerns removal of Governors.
7. Ninth Schedule: I. R. Coelho does not mean every Ninth Schedule law is automatically invalid.
8. Anti-Defection Law: Kihoto Hollohan recognised judicial review; it did not abolish the Speaker's adjudicatory role.
9. Legislative privileges: Sita Soren denied bribery immunity; it did not abolish legislative freedom of speech.
10. Same-sex relationships versus marriage: Navtej Singh Johar and Supriyo addressed different constitutional questions.
11. GST Council: Mohit Minerals clarified that Council recommendations are not legally binding.
12. Governor's assent: The 2025 Presidential Reference rejected judicially created deemed assent and generally prescribed judicial deadlines, while recognising limited intervention against prolonged unexplained inaction.

For Prelims, prioritise the Basic Structure cases, Fundamental Rights cases, reservation judgments, federalism and Governor judgments, election-related cases, and major judgments from 2022–2025. The most useful revision method is to connect each judgment with its constitutional article, its central holding, and whether that holding remains operative or was subsequently modified.

## Section A — Constitutional Amendments and Basic Structure

### Q1. Consider the following statements regarding *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973):

1. Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights under Article 368.
2. Parliament may destroy the basic structure if the amendment is passed unanimously.
3. The judgment was delivered by a 13-judge Bench.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

<div>
<strong>A.</strong> 1 and 2 only<br>
<strong>B.</strong> 2 and 3 only<br>
<strong>C.</strong> 1 and 3 only<br>
<strong>D.</strong> 1, 2 and 3
</div>

**Answer: C**

> **Explanation:** Statements 1 and 3 are correct. The judgment recognised Parliament's wide amendment power but prohibited destruction of the Constitution's basic structure, irrespective of the size of the parliamentary majority.

### Q2. Which judgment held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights, a position later superseded by the Basic Structure Doctrine?

<div>
<strong>A.</strong> *Golaknath v. State of Punjab*<br>
<strong>B.</strong> *Minerva Mills v. Union of India*<br>
<strong>C.</strong> *Waman Rao v. Union of India*<br>
<strong>D.</strong> *I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu*
</div>

**Answer: A**

> **Explanation:** *Golaknath* (1967) restricted Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights. *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) subsequently permitted such amendments subject to the basic-structure limitation.

### Q3. Consider the following statements regarding *Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980):

1. Limited constitutional amending power forms part of the basic structure.
2. Harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is constitutionally important.
3. The Court upheld the extension of Article 31C protection to all Directive Principles.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

<div>
<strong>A.</strong> 1 and 3 only<br>
<strong>B.</strong> 2 and 3 only<br>
<strong>C.</strong> 1, 2 and 3<br>
<strong>D.</strong> 1 and 2 only
</div>

**Answer: D**

> **Explanation:** Statements 1 and 2 are correct. The Court invalidated the 42nd Amendment's attempt to extend Article 31C protection to all Directive Principles.

### Q4. With reference to *I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu* (2007), which statement is correct?

<div>
<strong>A.</strong> Every law in the Ninth Schedule is automatically invalid.<br>
