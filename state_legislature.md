# State Legislature

## 1. Introduction

The State Legislature is the law-making organ of the State Government.

Under Article 168, the Legislature of a State consists of:

- The Governor.
- The Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha).
- The Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad), where one exists.

The Governor is an integral part of the State Legislature but is not a member of either House.

### Types of State Legislatures

| Type       | Composition                                           | Description |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------- |
| Unicameral | Governor + Legislative Assembly                       | One House   |
| Bicameral  | Governor + Legislative Assembly + Legislative Council | Two Houses  |

Unlike Parliament, where bicameralism is constitutionally established, a State may have either a unicameral or bicameral legislature.

## 2. States having Legislative Councils

Currently, six States have bicameral legislatures.

| State          | Legislative Assembly | Legislative Council |
| -------------- | -------------------- | ------------------- |
| Andhra Pradesh | Yes                  | Yes                 |
| Bihar          | Yes                  | Yes                 |
| Karnataka      | Yes                  | Yes                 |
| Maharashtra    | Yes                  | Yes                 |
| Telangana      | Yes                  | Yes                 |
| Uttar Pradesh  | Yes                  | Yes                 |

All other States have unicameral legislatures.

### Important points

- Jammu and Kashmir is presently a Union Territory, not a State.
- The former Jammu and Kashmir Legislative Council was abolished following the 2019 reorganisation.
- A Legislative Council is not a compulsory institution for every State.

## 3. Creation and abolition of Legislative Councils

### Article 169

Parliament may create or abolish a Legislative Council in a State through a simple majority.

However, Parliament can act only after the State Legislative Assembly passes a resolution requesting the creation or abolition.

### Majority required in the Legislative Assembly

The resolution for creation or abolition must be passed by:

1. A majority of the total membership of the Assembly; and
2. A majority of not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting.

This is a special majority.

### Procedure

1. The State Legislative Assembly passes the required resolution.
2. Parliament may enact a law creating or abolishing the Council.
3. The law may contain necessary provisions for amending the Constitution.

Important: Parliament is not legally bound to act merely because the Assembly has passed the resolution.

A law enacted under Article 169 is not deemed a constitutional amendment under Article 368.

### Prelims traps

- The Legislative Council cannot initiate the Article 169 resolution.
- The resolution alone does not create or abolish a Council.
- Parliament passes the law by the ordinary legislative procedure.
- The State Assembly's resolution requires the special majority described above.

## 4. Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha)

### Article 170

The Legislative Assembly is the Lower House of a bicameral State Legislature and the only House in a unicameral legislature.

Its members are directly elected by the people from territorial constituencies.

### 4.1 Composition

The Constitution ordinarily provides:

- Maximum strength: 500 members.
- Minimum strength: 60 members.

However, constitutional exceptions allow certain smaller States to have Assemblies with fewer than 60 members.

Examples include:

| State             | Assembly strength |
| ----------------- | ----------------- |
| Sikkim            | 32                |
| Goa               | 40                |
| Mizoram           | 40                |
| Arunachal Pradesh | 60                |

Other special constitutional arrangements also exist for certain States.

### 4.2 Reservation of seats

### Article 332

Seats are reserved in State Legislative Assemblies for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in accordance with constitutional provisions.

The reservation of seats for SCs/STs has been extended until 25 January 2030 through the 104th Constitutional Amendment.

The 106th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023 provides for reservation of approximately one-third of seats for women in State Legislative Assemblies, subject to its constitutional commencement and implementation conditions involving the relevant census and delimitation.

### 4.3 Duration of the Legislative Assembly

### Article 172

- Normal term: Five years from the date appointed for its first meeting.
- It may be dissolved before completion of its term.
- During a National Emergency, Parliament may extend its term by law for one year at a time.
- Such an extension cannot continue beyond six months after the Emergency ceases.

The Assembly is therefore not a permanent House.

### 4.4 Qualifications for membership

### Article 173

A person must:

1. Be a citizen of India.
2. Make the prescribed oath or affirmation before a person authorised by the Election Commission.
3. Be at least 25 years old.
4. Possess other qualifications prescribed by Parliament.

Prelims point: The oath taken as a candidate under Article 173 is distinct from the oath taken before sitting as a member under Article 188.

## 5. Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad)

### Articles 171 and 172

The Legislative Council is the Upper House of a bicameral State Legislature.

Unlike the Legislative Assembly, it is a permanent House.

### 5.1 Composition

### Article 171:

- Its total membership must not exceed one-third of the total membership of the Legislative Assembly.
- It must not ordinarily have fewer than 40 members.

Parliament may otherwise provide by law, and special statutory arrangements exist.

### 5.2 Election and nomination of members


| Category            | Proportion           | Electoral method                                                      |
| ------------------- | -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Local authorities   | About 1/3            | Elected by members of specified local authorities                     |
| MLAs                | About 1/3            | Elected by Assembly members from persons who are not Assembly members |
| Graduates           | About 1/12           | Elected by eligible graduates                                         |
| Teachers            | About 1/12           | Elected by eligible teachers                                          |
| Governor's nominees | Remainder, about 1/6 | Nominated by Governor                                                 |

The elected members are chosen through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote.

### 5.3 Governor's nomination

The Governor nominates persons with special knowledge or practical experience in:

- Literature.
- Science.
- Art.
- Co-operative movement.
- Social service.

Prelims trap: The Governor's nomination categories differ from those for the President's nomination of Rajya Sabha members, which do not expressly include the co-operative movement.

### 5.4 Duration

- The Legislative Council cannot be dissolved.
- Approximately one-third of its members retire every two years.
- The normal term of an elected or nominated member is six years.

### 5.5 Qualifications

A person must:

- Be an Indian citizen.
- Be at least 30 years old.
- Make the prescribed oath or affirmation as a candidate.
- Fulfil other qualifications prescribed by Parliament.

### Legislative Assembly versus Legislative Council

| Feature                             | Assembly               | Council                 |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------------------- | ----------------------- |
| House                               | Lower House            | Upper House             |
| Election                            | Direct                 | Indirect and nomination |
| Minimum age                         | 25 years               | 30 years                |
| Normal tenure                       | 5 years                | Permanent House         |
| Dissolution                         | Possible               | Not possible            |
| Money Bill                          | Can introduce and pass | Cannot introduce        |
| Council of Ministers responsible to | Assembly               | Not Council             |
| No-confidence motion                | Can pass               | Cannot remove ministry  |

## 6. Presiding officers

### 6.1 Legislative Assembly

### Articles 178–181

The Legislative Assembly elects:

- Speaker.
- Deputy Speaker.

Both are elected from among Assembly members.

### Speaker

The Speaker:

- Presides over Assembly sittings.
- Maintains order and discipline.
- Interprets procedural rules.
- Decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill under Article 199.
- Exercises powers under the Anti-Defection Law.

### Removal of Speaker and Deputy Speaker

A resolution must be passed by a majority of all the then members of the Assembly.

At least 14 days' notice is required.

The Speaker does not vacate office merely because the Assembly is dissolved; the Speaker continues until immediately before the first meeting of the new Assembly.

### 6.2 Legislative Council

### Articles 182–185

The Legislative Council elects:

- Chairman.
- Deputy Chairman.

Their removal also requires a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the Council, with at least 14 days' notice.

### 6.3 Voting rights of presiding officers

### Article 189:

- The Speaker or Chairman ordinarily does not vote in the first instance while presiding.
- They may exercise a casting vote in the event of a tie.

When a resolution for their own removal is under consideration, special provisions apply.

## 7. Sessions of the State Legislature

### Article 174

The Governor summons, prorogues and, where applicable, dissolves the Legislative Assembly.

### Important rules

- The Governor summons each House to meet at such time and place as considered appropriate under the Constitution.
- Not more than six months may intervene between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next session.
- The Governor may prorogue either House.
- The Governor may dissolve the Legislative Assembly, but not the Legislative Council.

The Governor ordinarily acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, subject to constitutional exceptions.

### Adjournment versus prorogation versus dissolution

| Feature         | Adjournment        | Prorogation    | Dissolution              |
| --------------- | ------------------ | -------------- | ------------------------ |
| Meaning         | Suspends a sitting | Ends a session | Ends the Assembly's life |
| Authority       | Presiding officer  | Governor       | Governor                 |
| House affected  | Either House       | Either House   | Assembly only            |
| Membership ends | No                 | No             | Yes                      |

Prelims point: Prorogation does not automatically cause pending Bills to lapse.

## 8. Governor's address and messages

### 8.1 Special address

### Article 176

The Governor addresses the Legislative Assembly or both Houses assembled together:

1. At the commencement of the first session after each general election to the Assembly.
2. At the commencement of the first session of each year.

The Governor informs the Legislature of the causes of its summons.

### 8.2 Messages

### Article 175

The Governor may:

- Address either House or both Houses assembled together.
- Send messages to either House concerning pending Bills or other matters.

Prelims trap: The Governor's annual special address is not required at the beginning of every session.

## 9. Quorum and voting

### Article 189

### Quorum

The quorum is:

- 10 members, or
- One-tenth of the total membership of the House,

whichever is greater.

Parliament may prescribe a different quorum by law.

### Voting

Ordinary questions are decided by a majority of members present and voting, unless the Constitution provides otherwise.

The presiding officer generally votes only in case of a tie.

### Example

If a Legislative Assembly has 200 members:

- One-tenth = 20.
- Required quorum = 20 members.

If an Assembly has 60 members:

- One-tenth = 6.
- Required quorum = 10 members.

## 10. Legislative procedure in States

### Articles 196–201

A State Legislature may pass:

1. Ordinary Bills.
2. Money Bills.
3. Financial Bills.

The procedure depends on the type of Bill and whether the State has a Legislative Council.

### 10.1 Ordinary Bills

An ordinary Bill may be introduced in either House of a bicameral State Legislature.

For enactment, it must pass through the applicable legislative process and receive the Governor's assent or the President's assent where constitutionally required.

### Powers of Legislative Council over ordinary Bills

If an ordinary Bill is passed by the Assembly and sent to the Council:

- The Council may delay it for up to three months on the first occasion.
- If the Assembly passes the Bill again and sends it to the Council, the Council may delay it for up to one month.

Thus, the Council may delay an Assembly-originated ordinary Bill for a maximum of approximately four months.

The Assembly ultimately prevails under Article 197.

Important: There is no joint sitting of the two Houses of a State Legislature.

### 10.2 Money Bills

### Articles 198 and 199

A Money Bill:

- Can be introduced only in the Legislative Assembly.
- Cannot be introduced in the Legislative Council.
- Requires the Governor's recommendation for introduction.
- Is certified as a Money Bill by the Speaker of the Legislative Assembly.

After the Assembly passes a Money Bill, it is sent to the Council for recommendations.

The Council must return it within 14 days.

The Assembly may accept or reject any recommendation.

If the Council does not return the Bill within 14 days, it is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form passed by the Assembly.

### Ordinary Bill versus Money Bill

| Feature                            | Ordinary Bill                | Money Bill                   |
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Introduction                       | Either House                 | Assembly only                |
| Council's role                     | Can delay up to 3 + 1 months | Can recommend within 14 days |
| Assembly's supremacy               | Yes, through Article 197     | Yes, through Article 198     |
| Joint sitting                      | No                           | No                           |
| Speaker's Money Bill certification | Not applicable               | Required                     |

### 10.3 Financial Bills

Not every Bill dealing with public money is a Money Bill.

A Financial Bill may include provisions that fall outside the strict definition of a Money Bill under Article 199.

Under Article 207:

- A Bill or amendment making provision for matters specified in Article 199(1)(a)–(f) requires the Governor's recommendation.
- A Bill containing such matters cannot be introduced in the Legislative Council.
- A Bill involving expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of the State cannot be passed unless the Governor has recommended its consideration.

Prelims trap: A Bill involving expenditure from the Consolidated Fund is not necessarily a Money Bill.

## 11. Governor's assent to State Bills

### Article 200

When a Bill passed by the State Legislature is presented to the Governor, the constitutional options include:

1. Assenting to the Bill.
2. Withholding assent.
3. Reserving the Bill for the President's consideration.
4. Returning a non-Money Bill for reconsideration in accordance with Article 200.

### 11.1 Reconsideration of a Bill

If the Governor returns a non-Money Bill:

- The Legislature reconsiders the Bill.
- It may pass the Bill again, with or without amendments.
- Article 200 provides that the Governor shall not withhold assent when the Bill is presented again.

The constitutional treatment of such Bills must also be understood in light of subsequent judicial interpretation, including the 2025 Presidential Reference concerning Articles 200 and 201.

### 11.2 Mandatory reservation for the President

The Governor must reserve a Bill for the President if, in the Governor's opinion, it would so derogate from the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position the Constitution intends the High Court to occupy.

Meaning: If a State Bill would seriously undermine the constitutional authority or independence of the High Court, the Governor must reserve it for presidential consideration.

### 11.3 President's consideration

Article 201

When a Bill is reserved for the President:

- The President may assent to it.
- The President may withhold assent.
- For a non-Money Bill, the President may direct the Governor to return it to the State Legislature for reconsideration.

If returned, the Legislature must reconsider the Bill within six months.

Even after reconsideration and passage, the President is not constitutionally bound to grant assent.

### Important judicial development

In its 2025 advisory opinion on the powers of the Governor and President regarding State Bills, the Supreme Court clarified that courts cannot impose universally binding timelines for assent where the Constitution provides none, or grant deemed assent in place of the constitutional authority. Limited judicial intervention may remain available against prolonged, unexplained inaction.

## 12. Lapsing of Bills in State Legislatures

### Article 196

The rules depend on the House in which the Bill is pending and whether the Legislative Assembly is dissolved.

### 12.1 When the Assembly is dissolved

| Situation                                                      | Does the Bill lapse? |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| Bill pending in the Legislative Assembly                       | Yes                  |
| Bill passed by Assembly but pending in Legislative Council     | Yes                  |
| Bill pending in Legislative Council and not passed by Assembly | No                   |
| Bill passed by both Houses and awaiting Governor's assent      | No                   |
| Bill reserved for President's consideration                    | No                   |

### 12.2 On prorogation

A Bill pending in the State Legislature does not lapse merely because a House is prorogued.

Prelims trap: Dissolution and prorogation have different effects on pending Bills.

## 13. State Budget and financial procedure

### Articles 202–207

### 13.1 Annual Financial Statement

### Article 202

The Governor causes the Annual Financial Statement, commonly called the State Budget, to be laid before the State Legislature.

It contains estimated receipts and expenditure for the financial year.

### 13.2 Charged and voted expenditure

Expenditure is divided into:

- **Charged expenditure:** Not submitted to the vote of the Legislative Assembly, though it may be discussed.
- **Voted expenditure:** Submitted to the Legislative Assembly through Demands for Grants.

Examples of charged expenditure include:

- Emoluments and allowances of the Governor.
- Salaries and allowances of the Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Legislative Assembly.
- Salaries and allowances of the Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Legislative Council, where applicable.
- Debt charges for which the State is liable.
- Salaries and allowances of High Court judges, as constitutionally provided.
- Other expenditure declared charged by the Constitution or State law.

### 13.3 Demands for Grants

### Article 203

- Demands for Grants are voted upon only by the Legislative Assembly.
- The Legislative Council cannot vote on Demands for Grants.
- A demand for a grant requires the Governor's recommendation.

### 13.4 Appropriation Bill

### Article 204

An Appropriation Bill authorises withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund of the State to meet approved expenditure.

No money may be withdrawn from the Consolidated Fund except under appropriation made by law, subject to constitutional provisions.

### 13.5 Supplementary, additional and excess grants

Article 205

| Grant               | Purpose                                                      |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Supplementary grant | Additional funds when authorised amount is insufficient      |
| Additional grant    | Expenditure for a new service not contemplated in the Budget |
| Excess grant        | Expenditure exceeding the authorised amount                  |

### 13.6 Vote on Account, Vote of Credit and Exceptional Grants

### Article 206

- **Vote on Account:** Advance grant to meet expenditure pending completion of the normal budget process.
- **Vote of Credit:** Grant for an unexpected demand of indefinite character or magnitude.
- **Exceptional Grant:** Grant forming no part of the current service of a financial year.

## 14. Legislative privileges

### Article 194

Members of State Legislatures enjoy certain privileges necessary for the independent functioning of the Legislature.

These include:

- Freedom of speech in the Legislature, subject to the Constitution and legislative rules.
- Immunity from court proceedings for anything said or any vote given in the Legislature or its committees.
- Other privileges recognised under the constitutional framework.

### Important distinction

Freedom of speech in the Legislature under Article 194 is different from the general freedom of speech under Article 19(1)(a).

In Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024), the Supreme Court held that legislative privileges do not protect legislators from prosecution for bribery connected with legislative speech or voting.

Prelims trap: Legislative privilege is not a blanket immunity from criminal law.

## 15. Disqualification of State Legislature members

Articles 190–193 and Tenth Schedule

### 15.1 Grounds for disqualification

Under Article 191, a member may be disqualified for:

- Holding an office of profit under the Union or State Government, subject to constitutional and statutory exceptions.
- Being of unsound mind and so declared by a competent court.
- Being an undischarged insolvent.
- Not being an Indian citizen or voluntarily acquiring foreign citizenship, or acknowledging allegiance to a foreign State.
- Being disqualified by a law made by Parliament.
- Defection under the Tenth Schedule.

### 15.2 Who decides disqualification?

| Ground                                | Deciding authority                                                     |
| ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Disqualification under Article 191(1) | Governor, acting according to the Election Commission's opinion        |
| Disqualification under Tenth Schedule | Speaker of Assembly or Chairman of Council, subject to judicial review |

### 15.3 Anti-Defection Law

The Tenth Schedule was introduced by the 52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985.

A member may be disqualified for voluntarily giving up party membership or violating a valid party direction in the circumstances prescribed by the Schedule.

Important distinctions:

- An independent member is disqualified if they join a political party after election.
- A nominated member may join a party within six months of taking their seat without attracting the specified disqualification.
- A qualifying merger requires agreement of at least two-thirds of the members of the legislature party.
- The earlier one-third split exception was removed by the 91st Amendment.

## 16. Legislative committees

State Legislatures use committees for detailed scrutiny of government expenditure, legislation and administration.

Important committees include:

| Committee                            | Principal function                                                         |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Public Accounts Committee            | Examines appropriation accounts, finance accounts and relevant CAG reports |
| Estimates Committee                  | Examines estimates and suggests economy and improvements                   |
| Committee on Public Undertakings     | Examines the working and accounts of specified public undertakings         |
| Committee on Government Assurances   | Reviews assurances given by ministers                                      |
| Committee on Subordinate Legislation | Scrutinises rules and regulations made under delegated legislative powers  |
| Business Advisory Committee          | Recommends allocation of time for legislative business                     |

Important: Membership, size, composition and procedures of these committees are governed by the rules and practices of the relevant State Legislature. They are not necessarily identical across States.

## 17. Legislative powers of State Legislatures

### Articles 245 and 246; Seventh Schedule

### 17.1 State List

State Legislatures ordinarily have exclusive power to legislate on State List subjects, subject to the Constitution.

Examples:

- Police.
- Public order.
- Public health.
- Agriculture.
- Local government.

### 17.2 Concurrent List

Both Parliament and State Legislatures may legislate on Concurrent List subjects.

Examples:

- Education.
- Forests.
- Marriage and divorce.
- Criminal law.
- Labour welfare.

### 17.3 When Parliament can legislate on State List subjects

| Article | Circumstance                                |
| ------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| 249     | Rajya Sabha resolution in national interest |
| 250     | National Emergency                          |
| 252     | Request by two or more States               |
| 253     | Implementation of international agreements  |
| 356–357 | Legislative powers during President's Rule  |

### 17.4 Conflict between Central and State laws

### Article 254

When a valid Central law and a State law conflict on a Concurrent List subject, the Central law ordinarily prevails.

However, a State law reserved for and receiving the President's assent may prevail in that State despite conflict with an earlier Parliamentary law.

Parliament may subsequently override such a State law.

## 20. Important UPSC Prelims traps

1. Governor is part of the State Legislature, even though not a member of either House.
2. A State Legislative Council is optional, unlike the Rajya Sabha.
3. Creation or abolition of a Legislative Council requires a special-majority Assembly resolution followed by a Parliamentary law.
4. A Legislative Council is a permanent House, with approximately one-third of members retiring every two years.
5. Minimum ages are 25 for Assembly and 30 for Council.
6. The Council cannot introduce Money Bills or vote on Demands for Grants.
7. A Council may delay an Assembly-originated ordinary Bill for three months plus one month.
8. There is no joint sitting of State Legislature Houses.
9. The Speaker certifies State Money Bills.
10. The Governor's special address occurs at the first session after a general election and the first session of each year.
11. Not more than six months may intervene between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next.
12. Quorum is 10 members or one-tenth of total membership, whichever is greater.
13. Dissolution causes certain Bills to lapse; prorogation does not.
14. A Bill pending in the Council but not passed by the Assembly does not lapse upon Assembly dissolution.
15. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible only to the Legislative Assembly.
16. For ordinary Article 191(1) disqualifications, the Governor acts according to the Election Commission's opinion; anti-defection cases are decided by the presiding officer, subject to judicial review.
17. Legislative privileges do not confer immunity for bribery.
18. The Legislative Council does not possess the same legislative powers as the Rajya Sabha.
19. A State Legislative Assembly cannot initiate a constitutional amendment Bill under Article 368.
20. State Legislatures participate in ratifying specified constitutional amendments when Article 368 requires ratification by at least half of the States.

### Q1. Consider the following statements about the State Legislature:

1. The Governor is a constituent part of the State Legislature.
2. Every State is constitutionally required to have a Legislative Council.
3. A Legislative Council is not subject to dissolution.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

<div>
<strong>A.</strong> 1 and 2 only<br>
<strong>B.</strong> 2 and 3 only<br>
<strong>C.</strong> 1 and 3 only<br>
<strong>D.</strong> 1, 2 and 3
</div>

<br>

**Answer: C**

> **Explanation:** Statements 1 and 3 are correct. Article 168 includes the Governor in the Legislature; a Council is optional and is a continuing House.

### Q2. Under Article 169, which of the following is required before Parliament can create a Legislative Council in a State?

<div>
<strong>A.</strong> A resolution of the Assembly passed by a majority of its total membership and at least two-thirds of members present and voting<br>

<strong>D.</strong> Ratification by at least half of the States

<br>

**Answer: A**

> **Explanation:** Article 169 requires the specified special-majority resolution of the Legislative Assembly; Parliament may then legislate.

3. Once the State Legislative Assembly passes the required resolution for creating or abolishing its Legislative Council, Parliament is constitutionally bound to enact the corresponding law.

### Q30. With reference to the Supreme Court's 2025 advisory opinion on the Governor's and President's powers regarding State Bills under Articles 200 and 201, which of the following statements is correct?

<div>
<strong>A.</strong> Courts may automatically treat a Bill as having received the Governor's assent if the Governor delays taking a decision.<br>
<strong>B.</strong> Courts cannot impose fixed deadlines for the Governor or President to decide on Bills when the Constitution does not prescribe such deadlines. Courts also cannot declare a Bill to have received assent merely because of a delay.<br>

<strong>D.</strong> The Governor may keep a Bill pending indefinitely without any possibility of judicial intervention.

> **Explanation:**
>
> In its 2025 advisory opinion, the Supreme Court clarified that:
>
> 1. **No judicially imposed fixed deadlines:** Articles 200 and 201 do not prescribe specific time limits for every decision on a Bill. Courts cannot create universally applicable deadlines that the Constitution itself does not prescribe.

### Q36. Consider a situation where Parliament has enacted a law on a subject in the Concurrent List. Subsequently, a State Legislature passes a law on the same subject, but some provisions of the State law conflict with the existing Parliamentary law.

### Under Article 254(2) of the Constitution, in which of the following circumstances can the conflicting State law prevail within that State?

<div>
<strong>A.</strong> When the Governor gives assent to the State Bill.<br>
<strong>B.</strong> When the State Legislative Assembly passes the Bill by a two-thirds majority.<br>
<strong>C.</strong> When the State Legislative Council approves the Bill.<br>
<strong>D.</strong> When the State Bill is reserved for the President's consideration and receives the President's assent, although Parliament can subsequently enact a law overriding the State law.
</div>

<br>

**Answer: D**

> **Explanation:**
>
> **Article 254(1) — General Rule**
>
> When a State law conflicts with a valid Parliamentary law on a Concurrent List subject, the Parliamentary law generally prevails, and the conflicting provisions of the State law become inoperative to the extent of the inconsistency.
>
> **Article 254(2) — Exception**
>
> A State law on a Concurrent List subject may prevail within that State despite conflicting with an earlier Parliamentary law if:
>
> 1. The State Bill is reserved for the consideration of the President.
> 2. The President gives assent to the Bill.
>
> However, Parliament retains the power to subsequently enact a law that adds to, amends, varies or repeals the State law.
>
> **Important Prelims points:**
>
> - The Governor's assent alone is insufficient to invoke Article 254(2).
> - A special majority in the State Legislature is not required.
> - Presidential assent does not permanently protect the State law against subsequent Parliamentary legislation.
> - The State law's precedence under Article 254(2) applies **only within the concerned State**.
